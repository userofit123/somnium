For most of the twentieth century, lucid dreaming had a credibility problem. A person wakes and says they knew they were dreaming. How could anyone check? The answer turned out to be the eyes.

## Why the eyes

During REM sleep, the stage in which most vivid dreaming occurs, the body's postural muscles are almost completely paralysed. The eye muscles and the muscles of breathing are spared [B1][B4]. If a dreamer looks left and right inside the dream, the real eyes move too, and the electro-oculogram records it. That makes eye movements a channel out of the dream [B15].

## Hull, 1975

On the morning of 12 April 1975, in the sleep laboratory at the University of Hull, Keith Hearne recorded the lucid dreamer Alan Worsley making a pre-arranged sequence of left-right eye movements during REM sleep [A34][A35]. This was the first objective signal sent from inside a dream. Hearne submitted his PhD thesis on the work to the University of Liverpool in 1978; it was not published as a journal article, which limited its visibility [A36][A16].

## Stanford, 1978 to 1981

Working independently, Stephen LaBerge signalled from his own lucid dream at Stanford on 13 January 1978, with EEG, eye movements and muscle tone all recorded [A35]. In 1981, with Lynn Nagel, William Dement and Vincent Zarcone, he published "Lucid dreaming verified by volitional communication during REM sleep", in which five subjects signalled lucidity by eye movements and fist clenches during unequivocal REM sleep [A38]. The signals had "observable physical concomitants" on the polysomnogram, which meant dream events could be time-stamped [B16].

Celia Green had predicted a decade earlier, from collected cases alone, that lucid dreams would be found to occur in REM sleep [A28]. The laboratory confirmed it.

## Who was first

Hearne later suggested that summaries he sent to Stanford in 1975 could have reached LaBerge; LaBerge has maintained that his work was independent, and no evidence has been produced that he saw Hearne's material. The two discoveries are generally treated as parallel, with Hearne first in time and LaBerge first in peer-reviewed print [A35]. Modern reviews credit both: "Hearne (1978) and LaBerge (1980) demonstrated that lucid dreams represent an objectively verifiable altered state of consciousness" [A21].

## The standard signal

The signal used today is a full-scale left-right-left-right sweep without pausing, written LRLR, which produces a distinctive trace [B15]. A "signal-verified lucid dream" is one in which the sleeper, in polysomnographically confirmed REM sleep, makes this pre-agreed movement. Laboratories use it to bracket events: in one study, dreamed hand clenching between two LRLR signals activated the sensorimotor cortex on the opposite side of the brain [B19].

## Two-way conversation

In 2021, four laboratories in the United States, Germany, France and the Netherlands went further. Sleepers in signal-verified lucid REM were asked questions by spoken words, Morse-coded lights or touch, and answered with eye movements or facial-muscle contractions. Across 36 participants and 57 sessions, 29 questions were answered correctly; in non-lucid REM control trials there was one correct answer among hundreds of no-response trials [A54][B27]. This is the strongest evidence that lucid dreamers can perceive outside information and reason in real time.

## What has not been shown

Verification shows that lucidity is real and occurs in REM sleep. It does not show that any particular technique works reliably, that brain stimulation can switch lucidity on, or that commercial devices do what they claim. Those questions are covered in the science and tools sections, where the evidence is thinner.
