Lucid dreaming is not rare. The best single estimate comes from a 2016 meta-analysis by Saunders, Roe, Smith and Clegg, who pooled 34 studies from 1966 to 2016 with 24,282 respondents. Lifetime prevalence, meaning at least one lucid dream ever, was 55 percent (95 percent confidence interval 49 to 62). Twenty-three percent (confidence interval 20 to 25) reported lucid dreams once a month or more [A21].

The authors add a memorable comparison: frequent lucid dreaming is about twice as common as left-handedness [A21].

## Representative samples

Surveys of students and online volunteers tend to run high; online samples in the meta-analysis averaged 65 percent lifetime prevalence [A21]. Representative samples are lower. In a representative German sample of 919 adults, 51 percent had experienced a lucid dream at least once and about 20 percent had them monthly or more [A60]. In the 2,929-person validation sample of the Mannheim Dream Questionnaire, 38.5 percent had never had a lucid dream, 10.5 percent had them about monthly and 3.2 percent almost every morning [B50].

## The definition matters

Since Snyder and Gackenbach's 1988 review, surveys distinguish lifetime prevalence from "frequent lucid dreamers", defined as one or more lucid dreams per month [A41][A21]. Van Eeden's original definition required full memory of waking life and the ability to act voluntarily; later researchers treat awareness alone as sufficient, and this difference still affects estimates [A21]. Unclear definitions can mislead up to a fifth of a sample, which is why good questionnaires give a short definition and sometimes an example [A21].

## Age

Lucid dreaming is "quite pronounced in young children". A survey of students aged 6 to 19 found that incidence drops sharply at about age 16, which the authors relate to the maturing of the frontal brain [A61]. In the meta-analysis, the child subgroup had the highest frequent-dreamer estimate, 26 percent [A21]. In adults, frequency declines with age [A60].

## Sex

Differences are small and inconsistent. The German representative sample found that women reported more lucid dreams than men [A60], but the meta-analysis found reporting of sex too patchy to analyse robustly [A21]. Dream recall, which is itself higher in women, explains part of any difference [A21].

## Dream recall

The strongest everyday correlate is how well you remember dreams. In the German sample, lucid dream frequency correlated strongly with dream recall frequency (r = .57) [A60]. The practical reading is that people who remember more dreams have more dreams in which lucidity can be noticed and reported, which is why every training programme begins with recall.

## Groups with unusually high rates

People with narcolepsy report far more lucid dreams: 77.4 percent versus 49.1 percent of controls had ever had one, and 7.6 versus 0.3 per month [B29]. Long-term meditators reported about two and a half times as many lucid dreams per month as non-meditators (1.11 versus 0.45), with no difference in dream recall [B46]. Frequent video-game players reported more lucid dreams than non-players in one correlational study [B47]. None of these findings show cause; they describe who tends to be lucid.

## What this means for you

If you have never had a lucid dream, you are in the minority, but not a small one. If you have them monthly, you are already a "frequent lucid dreamer" by the research definition. The app's onboarding asks the standard eight-point frequency question so that your starting point can be compared with published norms [B49][B50].
