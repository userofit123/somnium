Most of the evidence in this app comes from people who were not expert dreamers: students woken in sleep laboratories, and hundreds of volunteers keeping logbooks at home for two weeks. Their experience is a better guide to what you can expect than any famous dreamer's.

## Two weeks at home

In the Australian study, 169 people kept a baseline logbook for a week and then practised an assigned technique for a week. The group doing reality testing, wake-back-to-bed and MILD went from lucid dreams on 9.4 percent of nights to 17.4 percent; the reality-testing-only group showed no increase [D11]. When participants fell asleep within five minutes of finishing MILD, 45.8 percent of those attempts produced a lucid dream [B37].

In the international study, 355 people from 1,618 pre-tested, mostly in the United States, Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, Germany and Mexico, raised their lucid nights from 5.3 percent to 15.8 percent overall [B38]. Each day they logged the minutes spent on the technique, the number of repetitions, the minutes back to sleep and whether they fell asleep while still doing it [D12]. Better dream recall predicted success, and falling asleep within ten minutes gave 18.3 percent against 11.1 percent [B38]. Sleep quality did not suffer; on lucid nights it was slightly higher [B38]. On ordinary nights they recalled 1.7 dreams; on lucid nights 2.8 [C33].

Participants with insomnia were told to keep the lights off and use a quieter alarm [D12]. Those who added daytime reality tests did slightly worse than those who did not, and the author's conclusion was blunt: "RT appears to be an ineffective lucid dream induction technique - at least for short periods such as one week" [D12].

## One night in the laboratory

Erlacher and Stumbrys woke 51 unselected sport students from REM after about six hours of sleep. Those who spent an hour writing out their dream, marking dream signs, rehearsing MILD with the phrase "Next time I'm dreaming, I will remember to recognize that I'm dreaming" and explaining the technique back to the experimenter had self-rated lucid dreams in 54 percent of cases, 27 percent verified by eye signals, in the nap that followed. Thirty minutes gave 36 percent; an hour of reading fiction gave 9 percent; an hour of balance games gave none [D13][B39]. A follow-up that woke people after 4.5 hours instead found rates of 26 percent or less [D14].

## Twenty minutes with a sound

In Carr and colleagues' morning-nap study, participants spent 20 minutes before sleep pairing a sound and light cue with a lucid mindset. When the cue was replayed in REM, half had a signal-verified lucid dream, including three people who had never had one [B42]. At home with an app, 19 people went from 0.74 to 2.11 lucid dreams a week, and 14 of 19 had at least one, against 8 the week before; the cues also disrupted their sleep measurably [B43].

## Answering questions in a dream

Across 36 participants and 57 sessions in four laboratories, lucidity was signalled in 26 percent of sessions, and in about half of those episodes at least one question was answered correctly. Most questions got no response at all [B27][A54].

## What they found hard

The complaints match across studies and forums: staying awake too long and failing to get back to sleep [D12]; excitement ending the dream in its first seconds [D1][D5]; false awakenings mistaken for real ones [D19]; and, in the forum analysis, unpleasant lucid dreams, poor sleep and sleep paralysis after failed or forced attempts [B76].

## The useful summary

A motivated beginner doing MILD after a night awakening, and getting back to sleep quickly, can expect roughly one lucid dream per five or six attempts after a week of practice [B38]. That is the figure to hold on to. It is lower than the famous dreamers' records and higher than most people assume.
