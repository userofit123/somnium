Stephen LaBerge is unusual among researchers in that much of his evidence is his own dreaming, recorded night after night at Stanford and later at the Lucidity Institute. This article collects the parts of that record that this app relies on.

## The signal

LaBerge signalled from his own lucid dream on 13 January 1978, with EEG, eye movements and muscle tone all recorded [A35]. In the 1981 paper with Nagel, Dement and Zarcone, five subjects signalled lucidity by eye movements and fist clenches during unequivocal REM sleep, and the signals had "observable physical concomitants" on the recording [A38][B16].

## The MILD record

Before induction procedures he recalled fewer than one lucid dream a month. With autosuggestion, about five a month. With MILD, "as many as four lucid dreams in one night and as many as twenty-six in one month", and one or more lucid dreams on twenty of twenty-one laboratory nights [D1][D2]. The contrast between effortful intention and non-effortful suggestion, twenty of twenty-one nights against one of six, is why MILD rather than autosuggestion is the app's primary technique [D1].

## Spinning

From his dissertation record: spinning was used in 40 percent of 100 lucid dreams; new dream scenes resulted in 85 percent of these; lucidity persisted in 97 percent of the new dreams; the new scene almost always resembled his bedroom [D1]. Another dreamer arrived elsewhere in 5 of 11 spins [D1]. "I'm impressed by the power of expectation to determine what happens in my lucid dreams," he wrote, and the inability to spin anywhere but his bedroom is his own example of expectation fixing an outcome [D1].

## Losing and keeping lucidity

His loss-of-lucidity rate fell from 18 percent of lucid dreams in the first year to under 1 percent from the second year onwards, which he attributes to inner speech ("This is a dream") and practice [D1]. His lucid-dream rate rose during laboratory studies, "which incidentally illustrates the powerful effect of motivation" [D1].

## Text in dreams

"Every time I have tried this in my own lucid dreams the writing has mutated in some way," he writes of re-reading text, which is why he calls it the best reality test [D1]. The Lucidity Institute's figures are that text changes 75 percent of the time on one re-reading and 95 percent on two [D3].

## Other dreamers he recorded

LaBerge's book collects accounts from others, each a short lesson:

- Alan Worsley, the first person to signal from a lucid dream in a laboratory, on re-entry: "Lie very still - don't move a muscle! Relax and wait. The dream will return. I've had dozens of lucid dreams in a row with this method" [D1].
- Linda Magallon, on staying in the dream when vision faded: listening to voices, music or her breathing; conversation; rubbing or opening her dream eyes; touching her hands and face; touching objects; being touched; flying [D1].
- Scott Sparrow, who stabilised a shaking dream by looking at the ground and then a fixed star, and who argued that the dreamer's own body is one of the most stable reference points in a dream [D1][D38].
- Harold von Moers-Messmer, who first proposed looking at the ground [D1].

## The nap studies

In the Lucidity Institute's field studies, lucid dreams occurred in 27 percent of morning-nap dreams versus 1.3 percent of night dreams, and "nearly half (over 47%) of all lucid dreams ... took place in morning nap periods following 60 minutes of wakefulness" [D4]. These are the Institute's own participants, reported in its newsletter.

## How to read a self-record

A record like LaBerge's shows what is possible for a motivated, trained dreamer and gives the ideas that later studies tested. It is not a success rate for anyone else. The controlled studies in the science section, with hundreds of ordinary participants, give the rates to expect [B37][B38].
