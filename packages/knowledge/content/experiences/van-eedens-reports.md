Frederik van Eeden read "A Study of Dreams" to the Society for Psychical Research, and it was printed in the Society's Proceedings in 1913 [A19]. He had studied his own dreams between 20 January 1898 and 26 December 1912, recording the ones he considered most important [C6a].

## Naming the state

"The seventh type of dreams, which I called lucid dreams," he wrote, reporting "352 cases in the period between January 20, 1898, and December 26, 1912" [A19]. The sentence is the origin of the term that this app, and the whole research field, uses.

## The double awareness

Van Eeden describes an awareness that modern dreamers will recognise: the knowledge that "the physical body is sleeping and has quite a different position" from the body in the dream [A19][A20]. He was, in his own account, in two places at once, aware of both.

## The claret glass

His best-known passage is an experiment. He tried to shatter a claret glass with his fist and found that it broke only after a delay [A19][A20]. He treated the result as evidence that the dream world had its own physics, worth probing rather than merely enjoying. The habit of experimenting inside the dream, which the science section of this app describes in laboratory form, began here.

## A strict standard

Van Eeden's definition of a lucid dream required full memory of waking life and the ability to act voluntarily, not merely awareness [A21]. By that standard many of today's "semi-lucid" dreams would not count. Later researchers treat awareness alone as sufficient, and the difference still affects prevalence estimates [A21].

## Where it was heard

The paper was presented to a psychical research society rather than published in a psychology journal, and that placement kept it at the margins of the discipline [A23][A28]. It was Celia Green's 1968 survey that brought van Eeden's cases back into an academic frame, and the laboratories of the 1970s that proved what he had described [A28][A34][A38].

## Reading the original

The full text of "A Study of Dreams" is available online [A19]. The quotations above are short and attributed; the paper itself is a few dozen pages and rewards reading in full. Van Eeden's dreams are not instructions, but they are the earliest careful description, in English, of what the practice in this app leads to.
