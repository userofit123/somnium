## Origin

LaBerge's chapters on the building of dreams and on principles and practice [D1], and Tholey's taxonomy of manipulation [D8].

## Evidence level

Self-report and phenomenological study. Control is less common than awareness: 77 percent of a Brazilian sample had experienced lucidity but only 29 percent reported control [B31]. Lucid-dream intensity, including control, is inversely associated with depression, anxiety and stress, whereas frequency is not [B71].

## Expectation

LaBerge's spinning results led him to say "I'm impressed by the power of expectation to determine what happens in my lucid dreams": he could rarely spin anywhere except a scene resembling his bedroom [D1]. He recommends starting small: treat the scene as "modeling clay for the mind", change a detail, then more; or imagine the dream on a giant television with a remote [D1]. Tholey adds that inexperienced lucid dreamers cannot easily produce experiences that contradict waking experience [D7][D8].

## Two kinds of control

LaBerge distinguishes "magical manipulation" of the dream from self-control of your habitual reactions within it, and argues the latter transfers better to waking life [D1].

## Tholey's taxonomy of manipulation

By pre-sleep intention and autosuggestion; by wishing; by inner state ("If the dreamer courageously faced up to a threatening figure, its threatening nature in general gradually diminished and the figure itself often began to shrink"); by looking (figures lose their threat when looked in the eye); by verbal utterance (the question "Who are you?" changed figures of strangers into familiar individuals); by actions (spinning, flying, looking at the ground); and with the help of other dream figures [D8][D1]. His cautions: manipulation can be used as an escape, and aggressive actions were often followed by "avenging figures" [D7][D8].

## Steps: a first exercise

1. Once lucid and stable, pick one small change: the colour of an object, a door that opens onto somewhere new [D1].
2. Expect it rather than force it; look away and look back [D1].
3. If the dream resists, try a different route: walk through a door, spin while naming the target scene, or ask a dream figure for help [D1][D8].
4. Record what worked. The app keeps a control rating per dream so you can see it change [B22].

## Dream figures

Tholey asked nine experienced lucid dreamers to have dream characters draw, write, rhyme and solve arithmetic; some agreed and succeeded, "although the arithmetic accomplishments were poor". He concluded that nothing contradicts the assumption that dream characters have "consciousness in a specific sense" and that in therapy they should be treated as rational beings [D9]. He also describes an "internal self-helper" figure that can be requested by pre-sleep suggestion and tends to appear in high or brightly lit places [D8].

Robert Waggoner recommends addressing questions or requests to "the awareness behind the dream" rather than to figures or objects, and holds that the lucid dreamer directs the dream rather than controlling it; the exact wording of his five stages could not be verified and should be quoted from his book [D26].

## When

After your lucid dreams last long enough to try something; the stabilisation routine comes first. The app's progression puts planned dream tasks in the final phase [D1][D5].

## Common errors

- Trying large changes first; expectation fixes outcomes, so start small [D1].
- Fighting figures; attacking them made them regress and produced anxiety, guilt and avengers in Tholey's work [D8][D10].
- Impulsive flying, which often ends beginners' dreams [D1].
