## Origin

LaBerge observed in 1980 that "certain activities 'in the middle of the night'" (reading, writing, meditation, sex) were followed by lucid dreams, and concluded that "it is not the particular activity, but the alert wakefulness that facilitates lucid dreaming" [D4]. The Lucidity Institute formalised it as the morning-nap technique; the acronym WBTB is community usage [D3][D4].

## Evidence level

Controlled field studies and sleep-laboratory studies [D4][D11][D12][D13][D14]. The reviews treat WBTB as an amplifier whose effect cannot be separated from what you do while awake [B35].

## Why it works

Sleep cycles lengthen their REM periods across the night, so a return to sleep after five or six hours lands in long REM [B2][B3]. Waking also raises alertness immediately before REM and gives a window to rehearse an intention while recent dreams are fresh [B39].

## Steps

1. Sleep first. Set an alarm for about five to six hours after bedtime. Erlacher and Stumbrys woke participants from REM after at least 5 hours 40 minutes; waking after 4.5 hours in a follow-up gave much lower rates [D13][D14]. Aspy's studies used five hours [D11][D12]. Later is better if you can still return to sleep.
2. Get up. The course says: get out of bed, wash the face, "carefully test reality to make sure that you are actually awake", stretch [D5].
3. Do dream work. In every successful protocol the waking activity was about lucid dreaming: writing out the dream report, marking dream signs, reading about lucid dreaming, rehearsing MILD [D4][D11][D13]. Reading fiction for an hour gave 9 percent lucid dreams; playing balance games gave 0 percent [D13].
4. Choose a duration. LaBerge, Phillips and Levitan compared 10, 30 and 60 minutes awake before a morning nap: five times as many participants had lucid dreams after 60 minutes as after 10 [D4]. In the laboratory, 60 minutes beat 30 [D13]. Aspy's participants stayed up only as long as MILD took and still doubled or tripled their rate [D11][D12]. So 5 to 10 minutes is enough for MILD; longer raises success at the cost of sleep.
5. Return to bed and practise your technique (MILD or SSILD) until asleep [D11][D12].

## When

On nights when you can sleep in afterwards, ideally waking from a dream rather than from a time-based alarm [D13][D14]. Somnium suggests no more than two or three nights a week.

## Common errors

- Fully waking and failing to return to sleep; the international study told participants to keep lights off, use a quieter alarm and spend less time awake [D12].
- Doing nothing lucidity-related while up [D13].
- Waking too early in the night [D14].
- Doing WBTB every night and building sleep debt. LaBerge: "If you find you are losing sleep while struggling to have lucid dreams without result, let go of your efforts for a while" [D1].

## A caution

WBTB fragments sleep by design. Fragmented sleep is a known trigger for sleep paralysis, and the cautionary literature's main worry about lucid-dream practice is exactly this sleep disruption [B6][B69]. The safety section explains who should avoid it.
