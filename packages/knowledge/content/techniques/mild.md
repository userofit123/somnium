## Origin

Stephen LaBerge developed MILD during his doctoral work at Stanford and published it as a case study in 1980 [D2]. He revised it for the public in Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming (1990) [D1]. Before any induction procedure he recalled fewer than one lucid dream a month; with MILD he reported "as many as four lucid dreams in one night and as many as twenty-six in one month", and one or more on twenty of twenty-one laboratory nights [D1].

## Evidence level

Controlled field studies and sleep-laboratory studies. MILD with wake-back-to-bed raised lucid dreaming from 9.4 to 17.4 percent of nights in the Australian study [D11] and produced lucid dreams on 16.5 to 20 percent of nights in the international study [D12]. In the laboratory, an hour of dream work and MILD after about six hours of sleep gave self-rated lucid dreams in over half of participants, 27 percent verified by eye signals [D13]. Among cognitive techniques it has the most consistent supporting evidence [D15][B35].

## Prerequisite

LaBerge asks for good dream recall ("you should recall at least one dream per night") and a demonstrated ability to remember intentions while awake, for which he gives a one-week prospective memory exercise [D1].

## Steps, as LaBerge gives them

1. Set up dream recall. Before bed, resolve to wake up and recall dreams during each dream period of the night, or the first after dawn, or whenever convenient [D1].
2. Recall your dream. When you wake from a dream, recall as many details as possible. If you are so drowsy you are drifting back to sleep, do something to arouse yourself [D1].
3. Focus your intent. While returning to sleep, concentrate single-mindedly on your intention to remember to recognise that you are dreaming. Tell yourself: "Next time I'm dreaming, I want to remember I'm dreaming." Really try to feel that you mean it. If you find yourself thinking of anything else, let the thought go and return to the intention [D1].
4. See yourself becoming lucid. At the same time, imagine you are back in the dream you just woke from, but this time you recognise it is a dream. Find a dream sign in the experience; when you see it, say to yourself "I'm dreaming!" and continue the fantasy, for example imagining yourself taking off and flying [D1].
5. Repeat steps 3 and 4 until your intention is set, then let yourself fall asleep. If you find yourself thinking of anything else while falling asleep, repeat the procedure so that the last thing in your mind before sleep is the intention [D1].

LaBerge adds that if it takes a long time to fall asleep, do not worry: the longer you are awake, the more times you repeat the procedure. "If you are a very deep sleeper, you should get up after memorizing your dream and engage in ten to fifteen minutes of any activity requiring full wakefulness"; one of the best is to write out the dream and read it over, noting dream signs [D1]. The Lucidity Institute's version uses the phrase "Next time I'm dreaming, I will remember I'm dreaming", repeated "like a mantra" [D3].

## The study version

In the Australian study, participants set an alarm for five hours after bedtime, put a light on, sat up and recalled a dream, then lay down and repeated "next time I'm dreaming, I will remember that I'm dreaming", imagining being back in the recalled dream and noticing something bizarre that made them realise they were dreaming, until the intention was set or they fell asleep [D11].

## When

On waking from a dream in the last third of the night, or after a planned wake-back-to-bed awakening at about 4.5 to 6 hours [D11][D12][D13]. The course also teaches a bedtime version and warns that if you concentrate for more than 20 minutes without falling asleep you may let go, relax and keep the intention as your last thought [D5].

## The sleep-onset finding

The much-quoted "five-minute rule" is a post-hoc finding, not an instruction. Attempts in which participants fell asleep within five minutes of finishing MILD had a success rate of about 46 percent [D11]. In the larger replication, falling asleep within 10 minutes predicted success (18.3 versus 11.1 percent of nights), whereas the 5-minute cut-off did not reach significance [D12]. ## Common errors

- Repeating the phrase mechanically without the visualisation [D1].
- Trying MILD before dream recall is reliable, against LaBerge's stated prerequisite [D1].
- Staying awake so long that you cannot return to sleep; participants with insomnia were told to keep the lights off and use a quieter alarm [D12].
- Treating "five minutes" as a target to rush rather than a correlate [D11][D12].
