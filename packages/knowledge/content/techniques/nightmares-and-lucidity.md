## Origin

LaBerge's chapter on overcoming nightmares and Tholey's 1988 chapter on lucidity training as self-healing [D1][D10]. LaBerge's principle: "nothing can hurt you in dreams", so face what you were avoiding rather than escaping by waking [D1].

## Evidence level

Promising but thin. Reviews find that most studies report reduced nightmare frequency (one count: 10 of 11 positive), but samples are tiny, internal validity is limited, and lucidity may not be the active ingredient [B62][B63]. Imagery rehearsal therapy, which rewrites the nightmare while awake, remains the better-evidenced first-line treatment [B61]. The benefits section goes through the studies. This article covers what to do.

## Steps: conversing with dream characters

Adapted by LaBerge from Kaplan-Williams and Tholey [D1]:

1. Practise imaginary dialogues while awake with a recent unpleasant figure, using opening questions: "Who are you?", "What do you want from me?", "Can I help you?" [D1].
2. Set the intention to become lucid and to open a dialogue next time [D1].
3. In the dream, stay, face the figure, open a dialogue, address its problems and yours, try to reach agreement or make friends, then wake while you remember and write it down [D1].
4. Evaluate [D1].

Tholey found that conciliatory dialogue produced positive effects, that attacking figures made them regress (mother to witch to beast) and could produce anxiety, guilt and avengers [D1][D8]. His 1988 chapter presents "conciliatory interaction with threatening dream figures" as the most effective principle and reports a preliminary study of 62 students in which confronted figures lost their threatening character [D10]. LaBerge allows the conquest approach only in cases such as replayed abuse [D1].

## Steps: redreaming a recurrent nightmare

1. Record the nightmare in full [D1].
2. Choose a re-entry point and a new action [D1].
3. Relax and imagine the dream with the new action [D1].
4. Evaluate [D1].
5. If the nightmare recurs, carry out the plan while lucid [D1].

This is close to imagery rehearsal therapy, with lucidity as the optional final step. Several responders in the clinical trials never became lucid and still improved, which suggests the rehearsal does much of the work [B57].

## What to say

Tholey's recommended opening questions are "Who am I?" and "Who are you?" [D1]. LaBerge's broader advice is to seek the highest value in the dream [D1]. A phrase sometimes attributed to LaBerge, "look for the self", could not be found in his text [D1].

## When

Nightmare work is best done with a therapist if you are in acute depression, grief or post-traumatic stress [B60][B73]. For ordinary bad dreams, start with the waking dialogue practice and the redreaming exercise; lucidity, if it comes, is a bonus.

## Common errors

- Escaping by waking, which produces false awakenings and leaves the fear unaddressed [D1][D10].
- Fighting, which breeds avengers [D8].
- Waiting for lucidity before doing anything; the rehearsal works awake [B57][B61].
