## Origin

The Lucidity Institute's NightLight studies of the early 1990s, by LaBerge, Levitan and colleagues [D4]. The course's Unit 4 turns it into an exercise [D5].

## Evidence level

Controlled field studies by one group [D4]. The rates are the highest reported for any cognitive protocol, but they come from the Institute's own newsletter series and have not been independently replicated in the same form.

## What was found

- Naps were ten times more likely than the preceding night to contain lucid dreams [D4].
- "Get Up Early, Take a Nap, Be Lucid" (Levitan 1991) found 67 percent of participants had a lucid dream in a 90-minute nap taken 90 minutes after waking 90 minutes early, versus 8 percent in the night [D4].
- Morning naps beat afternoon naps in 9 of 11 participants [D4].
- LaBerge, Phillips and Levitan (1994) compared 10, 30 and 60 minutes awake, reading about lucid dreaming, before a 90-minute morning nap in 22 participants: lucid dreams occurred in 27 percent of nap dreams versus 1.3 percent of night dreams; five times as many participants had lucid dreams after 60 minutes awake as after 10; "nearly half (over 47%) of all lucid dreams ... took place in morning nap periods following 60 minutes of wakefulness". They did not test longer wake periods [D4].

The Institute's FAQ summarises: wake an hour earlier than usual, stay awake 30 to 60 minutes reading about lucid dreaming and reality checking, then practise MILD while falling asleep; "one study showed a 15 to 20 times increased likelihood of lucid dreaming" [D3]. The widely quoted "30 to 120 minute" window is a later authors' summary of this literature [D13].

## Steps, from the course

1. Wake 90 minutes earlier than usual [D5].
2. Get up and be active for 90 minutes; avoid heavy caffeine [D5].
3. Return to bed and set an alarm for 90 to 120 minutes [D5].
4. Practise the modified MILD until asleep [D5].
5. Log night and nap dreams [D5].

A shorter version, from the FAQ: 30 to 60 minutes up instead of 90 [D3].

## Why it works

A morning nap after a short period of wakefulness enters REM quickly and the REM is long, because the drive to enter REM is strongest in the late morning [D5]. Add an hour of alert, lucidity-focused wakefulness and you have every ingredient the laboratory studies found to matter [D13].

## When

On free days. The protocol costs 90 minutes of extra bed time plus the early rise, so it suits weekends rather than working nights. The app's progression suggests it as an alternate-night option in the later weeks, no more than three or four times a week unless sleep remains good [D1].

## Common errors

- Spending the wake period on anything other than dream work; in the laboratory, an hour of fiction gave 9 percent and games gave 0 percent [D13].
- Napping in the afternoon, which was less effective in most participants [D4].
- Napping without MILD; the nap gives the REM, the intention gives the lucidity [D5].
