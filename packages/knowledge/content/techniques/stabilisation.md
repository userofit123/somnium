## Origin

LaBerge's chapter on principles and practice in Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming, the Lucidity Institute course's "Traveler's Guide", and Tholey's manipulation studies [D1][D5][D8].

## Evidence level

LaBerge's own dissertation record for spinning, Aspy's use of hand-rubbing as the standard stabiliser in two large studies, and Tholey's phenomenological findings [D1][D11][D8]. No controlled trial compares stabilisers.

## The principle

All the techniques "are based on the idea of loading the perceptual system so it cannot change its focus from the dream world to the waking world" [D1]. The first sign of fading is usually visual: loss of colour and detail, dimming [D1].

## Steps: the first seconds

1. Keep cool, calm and collected. Restrain the impulse to jump and shout; "imagine what it's like to be dealt a winning hand in poker" [D5]. "Novice lucid dreamers often wake up the moment they become lucid" [D1].
2. Do a reality test to confirm [D11].
3. Engage the dream: look, listen, feel [D5].
4. Move around, run, fly, dance, while observing [D5].
5. Rub your palms together vigorously, focusing on the sensations while repeating "this is a lucid dream" [D11].

## Steps: spinning, when the dream fades

1. Notice when the dream begins to fade; vision goes first [D1].
2. As soon as it fades, "before the feel of your dream body evaporates, stretch out your arms and spin like a top"; you must vividly feel the spinning, not imagine it [D1].
3. While spinning, remind yourself that the next thing you see, touch or hear will probably be a dream [D1].
4. Test your state wherever you arrive [D1].

LaBerge's record: spinning used in 40 percent of 100 lucid dreams; new dream scenes resulted in 85 percent of these; lucidity persisted in 97 percent of the new dreams; the new scene almost always resembled his bedroom [D1]. He found "both falling backward and spinning in the dream to be especially effective" [D1]. Spinning can also take you to a chosen scene by repeating a target phrase while spinning [D1].

## Other stabilisers

- Inner speech: repeat "This is a dream" or "I'm dreaming", silently if possible. LaBerge's own loss-of-lucidity rate fell from 18 percent of lucid dreams in year one to under 1 percent from year two onwards [D1].
- Touch and sound: Linda Magallon's list of actions that prevented awakenings when vision faded: listening to voices, music or her breathing; conversation; rubbing or opening her dream eyes; touching her hands and face; touching objects; flying [D1].
- Looking at the ground or the hands: Harold von Moers-Messmer first proposed looking at the ground; Sparrow stabilised a shaking dream by looking at the ground and then a fixed star; Castaneda's hands serve the same purpose [D1]. But Tholey found that fixating a stationary point blurs it and ends the dream within about ten seconds, so look at details and keep the eyes moving [D7][D8].
- Rapid eye or body movements when the dream threatens to end (Tholey) [D8].
- Shouting "increase clarity": Tholey lists verbal utterances among manipulation methods, but the specific command is community practice with no originator [D1][D8].

## Staying lucid versus staying asleep

The course notes the "other edge of the sword": too much involvement makes you forget you are dreaming. It has you review five past lucid dreams for the events that preceded loss of lucidity, your "danger zones", and plan reminders [D5].

## When

Practise spinning while awake first, as the course instructs, so the movement is automatic [D5]. Then use the first-seconds routine in every lucid dream.

## Common errors

Excitement; impulsive flying, which often ends beginners' dreams; staring at one point; trying to escape a frightening scene by waking, which produces false awakenings [D1][D5].
