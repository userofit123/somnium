## Origin

LaBerge coined "dreamsign" for the peculiar, recurring features of your own dreams that can serve as cues to recognise the dreaming state. The inventory and the cataloguing exercise are in Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming (1990) and the Lucidity Institute's course (1995) [D1][D3][D5]. The book's own example is a "mutant lens" that kept multiplying in LaBerge's dreams: "I have become lucid in at least a dozen dreams by recognizing this particular oddity" [C1].

## Evidence level

A component of MILD and of the DreamLight studies; no isolated controlled test [D1][D3]. The Lucidity Institute reported from an in-house survey that "people were more likely to reach lucidity in a dream if they showed a high awareness of the strangeness of dreamsigns" [C2]. That is a newsletter study, not a peer-reviewed trial [C2].

## The four categories

The inventory has four primary categories [C1]:

- Inner awareness: "You have a peculiar thought, a strong emotion, feel an unusual sensation, or have altered perceptions." Example: "Somehow I could see perfectly without my glasses."
- Action: you, another character or an object "do something unusual or impossible in waking life", including malfunctioning devices. Example: "The car accelerates dangerously, and the brakes don't work."
- Form: a shape "is oddly formed, deformed, or transforms", including odd clothing and hair, and settings that differ from waking life. Example: "Her face changes as I look at her."
- Context: "The place or situation in the dream is strange." Example: "My brother, who is dead, was in the kitchen with me."

Context signs often consist of ordinary elements in odd combination: "there is nothing inherently strange about any item by itself" [C1]. The dream-signs section has the full table with sub-types.

## Steps: cataloguing your dream signs

1. Keep a dream journal. "When you have collected at least a dozen dreams, proceed to the next step" [C1].
2. Catalogue your dream signs. "Mark the dreamsigns in your dream reports. Underline them, and list them after each dream description" [C1].
3. Classify each dream sign by category; "if you dreamed of a person with the head of a cat, this would be a form dreamsign" [C1].
4. Pick your target. "Count how many times each dreamsign category ... occurs and rank them by frequency. Whichever occurs most often will be your target dreamsign category" [C1].
5. Practise looking for dream signs while awake. "Make a habit of examining your daily life for events that fit under your dreamsign category" [C1].

The course refines this: read your last ten dreams, list each sign with its category, select two sub-categories as targets, and for a week or ten dreams rate each target sign on the 0 to 4 Dreamsign Awareness Scale [C2]. It also adds a "Waking Life Dreamsigns" list: carry a notebook and record waking anomalies that resemble your targets [C2].

## How the app uses this

The app marks candidate signs in each entry for you to confirm, stores both category and sub-type, counts and ranks them, and shows the just-written dream's signs on the MILD rehearsal screen, as LaBerge's commentary recommends [C1]. It also lets you attach a reminder to a habitual moment linked to a sign, following the "Joe Dreamer" example of testing when stepping into an elevator or going to the bathroom [C1].

## When

Start after a dozen recorded dreams [C1]. The targeting then feeds MILD (step 4, "find a dreamsign in the experience") and reality testing (test "any time you come in contact with something that resembles a dreamsign") [C1].

## Common errors

- Marking only spectacular oddities and missing the quiet context signs, which are the commonest kind [C1].
- Choosing a target by appeal rather than by count; the rule is frequency, with appeal only as a tie-breaker [C1].
- Treating a recurrent dream (the same story repeated) as a dream sign (the same element across different dreams); both matter and the app records them separately [C17].
