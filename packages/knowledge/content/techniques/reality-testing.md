## Origin

Paul Tholey's reflection technique (1959) is the root [D7][D8]. LaBerge's "critical state testing" is an explicit adaptation; the specific tests of reading twice, checking a digital watch and trying to fly come from him [D1][D3].

## Evidence level

Field studies, and the news is sobering. Reality testing on its own did not increase lucid dreaming in one week in the Australian study [D11], and in the international study the author concludes "RT appears to be an ineffective lucid dream induction technique - at least for short periods such as one week" [D12]. Daily test counts were uncorrelated with lucid dreams, and adding reality testing to MILD made things slightly worse [D12][B38]. Reality testing was ineffective in five of six recent studies that included it, except when paired with sound cues during sleep [B35][B44]. Tholey and LaBerge both treat it as most useful for beginners and in combination with intention [D1][D8]. This app frames it as a low-cost habit that supports recognition, especially after false awakenings, not as an inducer by itself [B35].

## Steps, as LaBerge gives them

1. Plan when to test your state. Pick five to ten occasions during the day that resemble your dreams: contact with a dream sign; anything surprising, unlikely, emotionally intense or dreamlike; recurrent-dream situations. LaBerge's example list: stepping into an elevator, speaking to the boss, seeing an attractive woman, reading a typographical error, going to the bathroom [D1].
2. Test your state. Ask "Am I dreaming or awake?" "Don't just automatically ask the question and mindlessly reply, 'Obviously, I'm awake,' or you will do the same thing when you actually are dreaming." Look for oddities; think back over the last several minutes and check whether you have trouble remembering what just happened [D1].

## Which tests

- Read text twice: find writing, read it, look away, read it again. "Every time I have tried this in my own lucid dreams the writing has mutated in some way." The Lucidity Institute states that text in dreams changes 75 percent of the time on one re-reading and 95 percent on two [D1][D3].
- Digital watch or clock: look at it twice; "in a dream, it will never behave correctly". Analog watches can tell plausible dream time [D1].
- Try to fly: hop and try to prolong time off the ground [D1].
- Memory test (Tholey): try to remember what preceded the present situation; gaps or bizarre events indicate a dream. Useless after a false awakening, hence the next test [D7][D8].
- Light switch (Tholey): on any apparent awakening, turn a light on; if it does not go on, suspect a dream [D8].
- Turning test (Tholey): turn 180 degrees and try to stand still [D7][D8].
- Nose-pinch breathing and finger through palm: the two tests used in Aspy's studies; they come from community practice, not from LaBerge [D11][D12].
- Pinching yourself is explicitly rejected: it just produces a convincing pinch [D1].
- Counting fingers and jumping are widely used community checks with no identifiable originator [D1].

## When and how often

LaBerge: at least the five to ten selected occasions [D1]. Tholey: "as often as possible", in dreamlike situations, and ideally just before falling asleep, especially in the early morning hours [D8]. Aspy: a minimum of ten a day [D11][D12]. The Lucidity Institute course assigns eight planned occasions per day [D5].

## The genuine question

All three sources insist on it. Tholey's whole method rests on a "critical-reflective attitude"; with practice "the posing of critical questions [becomes] a superfluous matter" because the disposition itself does the work [D8]. LaBerge's "last word in state testing": "Anytime you find yourself seriously suspecting that you just might be dreaming, you probably are!" [D1].

## Common errors

- Mechanical checks with an automatic "obviously I'm awake", which train the same response in the dream [D1][D8][D11][D20].
- Treating the count of checks as the goal; the count did not predict lucid dreams [D12].
- Skipping the imagination step. LaBerge adds: "After having satisfied yourself that you're awake, tell yourself, 'Okay, I'm not dreaming, now. But if I were, what would it be like?'" [C1].
