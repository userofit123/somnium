## Origin

LaBerge's DreamLight studies of the late 1980s used light, sound and vibration as "artificial dreamsigns": a taped "This is a dream" produced 5 lucid dreams in 15 REM-period trials but woke subjects 8 times; vibration cues after daytime training gave 11 of 18 subjects lucid dreams [D1]. The modern form, "targeted lucidity reactivation", comes from Karen Konkoly, Ken Paller and colleagues [B42][B43].

## Evidence level

Replicated in two laboratories, with one app study [B42][B43][B44]. This is the only peer-reviewed evidence for any lucid-dreaming app; commercial app claims beyond it are unverified [B43].

## What the studies found

- Carr and colleagues paired a sound and light cue with a lucid mindset for 20 minutes before a morning nap, then replayed the cue in REM: 50 percent of participants had a signal-verified lucid dream in a single nap, including three with no prior lucid dreams [B42].
- Erlacher and colleagues paired ring tones with reality testing in the laboratory: 5 self-rated lucid dreams versus 1 under sham, 2 signal-verified [B44].
- An olfactory version yielded 2 of 16 and was judged unpromising [B45].
- Konkoly and colleagues ported the method to an Android app that trains the cue before bed and replays it from 6 hours after training. In the first experiment (19 people), lucid dreams rose from 0.74 to 2.11 per week. In the second (120 people), the group that kept receiving trained cues held its rate while groups receiving untrained or no cues declined. Cues produced measurable sleep disruption on accelerometry, and the authors flag expectancy and arousal as alternative explanations [B43].

## Steps

1. Choose a distinct sound [B43].
2. Before sleep, train the association for about 20 minutes: each time the sound plays, do a reality test with a genuine question and remind yourself, "when I hear this in a dream, I will know that I am dreaming" [B42][D5].
3. Have the sound replayed softly during the likely REM periods: in the app study, from about 6 hours after training [B43].
4. On waking, record whether the cue appeared in the dream and whether you recognised it [D1].

Daytime training is essential. LaBerge found that the light cue was woven into the dream 80 percent of the time, so "mental preparation to recognise it" is what turns a noise into a cue [D1].

## When

After five or six hours of sleep, on nights you can afford some disruption. The app's version is off by default and limited to a window you choose.

## Common errors

- Playing cues without the training session; untrained cues did nothing in the app study [B43].
- Volume high enough to wake you; the DreamLight sound cue woke subjects more often than it made them lucid [D1].
- Using it every night; the cues disrupted sleep measurably [B43].

## What this is not

Consumer masks and headbands make similar claims without comparable evidence; the tools article covers them [B56]. Sound cues are a reminder, not a switch.
