## Origin

LaBerge, Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming, where it is a stated prerequisite for MILD; extended as the "I Remember" game in the Lucidity Institute course [D1][D5].

## Evidence level

The rationale comes from prospective-memory research; the exercise has no isolated test [D1]. Its logic is that MILD is a prospective-memory task: "remember to remember" that you are dreaming. LaBerge requires "a demonstrated ability to remember intentions while awake" before starting MILD [D1].

## Steps

1. Each morning, read only that day's four target events and memorise them. LaBerge's example for one day: next time I see a pet or animal; next time I look at my face in a mirror; next time I turn on a light; next time I see a flower [D1].
2. During the day, when you notice the next occurrence of each target, perform a state test: "Am I dreaming?" [D1].
3. At the end of the day, record how many of the four targets you hit. A target noticed only on a later occurrence counts as a miss [D1].
4. Continue for at least a week. If you still miss most targets, continue with your own lists until you hit most of them [D1].

## When

In the weeks before you start MILD, alongside dream recall. The app's recommended progression places it in the dream-sign and state-testing phase, with "hitting most daily memory targets" as the metric to advance [D1][D5].

## Common errors

- Reading the whole week's targets at once instead of one day at a time [D1].
- Counting a late notice as a hit [D1].
- Forgetting to do the state test when a target appears; the test is the point, not the noticing [D1].

## Related ideas

Castaneda's "look at your hands" works, if it works, as a prospective-memory cue: resolve to find your hands, notice them, test your state [A32][D25]. The Lucidity Institute's "Waking Life Dreamsigns" list, in which you record waking anomalies that resemble your targets, is the same skill applied to your own signs [C2].
