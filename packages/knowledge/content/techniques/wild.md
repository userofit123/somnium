## Origin

A wake-initiated lucid dream is entered "directly from the waking state, without passing through other stages of sleep and with unbroken awareness" [D5]. Tholey classified such methods as "techniques allowing the subject to maintain lucidity ... while one is falling asleep" [D7]. LaBerge groups them by what you attend to: hypnagogic imagery, a visualisation, a repetitive mental task, or the body [D1].

## Evidence level

Case reports and Tholey's phenomenological studies; no controlled induction trial isolates WILD [D13][B15]. Lucid dreams entered from light sleep were confirmed by eye signals in LaBerge's early work [B15]. Erlacher and Stumbrys note that in their laboratory the lucid dreams after wake-back-to-bed were mostly dream-initiated, even when participants tried to stay aware [D13]. Treat WILD as advanced and anecdotal in efficacy; it is the route most likely to pass through sleep paralysis and hypnagogic imagery [B15].

## What to expect

The physiological state is a waking, active brain with a body in REM paralysis. Reported sensations include being unable to move, weights on the chest, "electricity", vibrations, loud buzzing or roaring, body parts melting, and the feeling of leaving the body. "Sleep paralysis can be quite terrifying, but it is harmless." Panic "is likely to stimulate the limbic (emotional) areas of the brain and cause the REM state to persist". Relax, remember you are in the state you are in every night during REM, and treat it as the threshold of a dream: "Just step over and you're in the world of lucid dreams" [D1].

## Steps: the hypnagogic imagery technique (LaBerge)

1. Relax completely: lie in bed, close the eyes, relax head, neck, back, arms and legs, breathe slowly; use progressive relaxation or the 61-point relaxation if needed [D1].
2. Observe the visual images. Focus gently on the images that appear before the mind's eye; watch how they begin and end; observe "as delicately as possible", without holding on, as a detached observer. Fleeting patterns gradually develop into scenes [D1].
3. Enter the dream. When the imagery becomes a moving, vivid scenario, "allow yourself to be passively drawn into the dream world. Do not try to actively enter the dream scene." Beware "too much involvement and too little attention" [D1].

Tholey, quoted by LaBerge: "It is not desirable to want actively to enter into the scenery, since such an intention as a rule causes the scenery to disappear." He also recommends resolving beforehand to carry out a particular action in the dream, so that if you lose lucidity on entry you can regain it [D1].

## Other anchors

- Count yourself to sleep: as you drift off, count "1, I'm dreaming; 2, I'm dreaming ...", keeping a degree of vigilance; at some point you will be saying "I'm dreaming" and notice that you are [D1].
- Body-focused: after waking from a dream, lie on the back or right side, pass attention through the body, watch for vibrations and body-image distortions, "the harbingers of REM sleep paralysis"; when paralysis is complete, imagine your dream body floating, rolling or sitting up out of the physical body. Then, LaBerge insists, do state tests: everything, including the bed, is a dream [D1].
- Tibetan visualisations at the throat, adapted by LaBerge, which the traditions article describes [D1][D30].
- A mantra or affirmation is simply the counting technique with a phrase [D1][D32].

## When

After waking in the last third of the night, or in a morning nap, when the drive to enter REM is strongest [D5][D4]. Not at bedtime: the first REM period is short and far away, and SSILD's author calls bedtime attempts "ineffective because early sleep is mostly NREM" [D19].

## Common errors

- Forcing entry; active wanting makes the scenery disappear [D1].
- Panicking at paralysis [D1].
- Trying at bedtime [D19].
- Practising so long that sleep is lost [D19].
- Moving on awakening instead of lying still, which loses the chance of re-entry [D1].
