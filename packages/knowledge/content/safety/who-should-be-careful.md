Lucid dreaming is an ordinary experience that most people have had. Deliberately cultivating it is a different matter: the main methods interrupt sleep, and the long-term effects of practising a hybrid state of consciousness are unknown [B69]. This page sets out who the research suggests should hold back or seek advice first. It is not medical advice.

## What the cautionary literature says

Vallat and Ruby (2019) argued that cultivating lucidity is not obviously benign, because the main methods fragment sleep and because lucid REM is a hybrid state whose long-term effects are unknown [B69]. Soffer-Dudek (2020) called the field's focus on benefits a shared blind spot and asked for work on harms, particularly in people high in baseline dissociation or schizotypy [B70].

The key empirical study is Aviram and Soffer-Dudek (2018), with 187 students, 78 followed for two months. Spontaneous lucid-dream frequency was unrelated to psychopathology. Deliberate induction attempts correlated with stress, sleep problems, obsessive-compulsive symptoms and dissociation; among the 64 people using induction techniques, technique use correlated with sleep problems (r = .37), schizotypy (r = .34), depression (r = .35) and dissociation (r = .32). Over two months, the frequency of induced, not spontaneous, lucid dreams predicted increases in dissociation and schizotypy, controlling for baseline [B71]. The effects are small, self-reported and from one student sample, but the direction is consistent [B71].

## Psychosis and bipolar disorder

Mota and colleagues (2016) compared 25 people with schizophrenia, 20 with bipolar I disorder and 28 controls. Lifetime lucid-dream prevalence was similar across groups, but among lucid dreamers, dream control was reported by 67 and 73 percent of the clinical groups versus 23 percent of controls. The authors warn that lucid-dream practice could "further empower deliria and hallucinations" in psychotic populations [B72]. Holzinger's clinical group likewise advises caution with psychotic clients [B70].

## The list

Based on the above, this app advises against induction practice, or recommends discussing it with a clinician, if you:

- have a psychotic disorder or bipolar disorder, or a history of psychotic episodes [B72];
- have a dissociative disorder or marked derealisation or depersonalisation [B71][B70];
- have an active sleep disorder (insomnia, untreated sleep apnoea, narcolepsy under treatment) or are already sleep-deprived [B69];
- are in acute depression, grief or PTSD without clinical support; nightmare work is best done with a therapist [B60][B73];
- are under 18, pregnant, or have cardiac, respiratory or seizure conditions, if any substance is contemplated [B52][B56].

## Sleep paralysis and distressing lucid dreams

Fragmented sleep is a known trigger for sleep paralysis, which is unpleasant but physiologically harmless and self-limiting [B6][B7]. A content analysis of 400 forum posts found the main complaints were lucid dysphoria, poor sleep and sleep paralysis, mostly after failed induction or low-control lucid dreams [B76]. If you have a history of frightening sleep paralysis, the wake-initiated methods are the ones most likely to pass through it [B15].

## The stop rule

If lucid dreams become distressing, if waking reality starts to feel dreamlike, or if you notice increasing detachment, pause the practice and talk to a professional [B71][B70]. The app repeats this rule in Train, and you can switch all induction features off in one place.
