These are contemplative paths whose stated goal is recognising the dreamlike nature of all experience, not lucid dreaming for its own sake. Most teachers state that the practices are meant to be received from a qualified teacher [D30][D29]. This app presents them as the traditions describe them, labelled as tradition rather than evidence. A 2026 paper cautions that dream yoga and lucid dreaming share a core skill but differ in aim [A14].

## The Six Yogas of Naropa: milam

Dream yoga is one of the Six Dharmas of Naropa in the Kagyu and Gelug schools [D30].

Gampopa's stages: (1) seizing the dream: by day regard all perceptions as dreamlike, at night resolve firmly to recognise dreams; (2) training: within the dream face whatever arises without fear, entering water, flying, while knowing it is a dream; (3) blessing as illusory and removing fear: check for fear and release it by recognising the dream; (4) meditating on reality: analyse all dream experience as one's own unborn mind [D30].

Tsongkhapa's four trainings: retaining awareness in dreams; controlling and increasing dream content; overcoming fear and understanding the illusory nature; meditating on the suchness of dreams. Prerequisites are the clear-light sleep practice, tummo and illusory body [D30].

Posture and visualisation: lie on the right side in the lion posture with strong resolve; visualise a lotus at the throat with syllables (Gampopa: five syllables, moving attention from one to the next while falling asleep; Tsongkhapa: a small red four-petalled lotus with Ah or Om at the centre); meditate by day "on the chakras, especially that at the throat"; if recognition is too hard, move attention to the crown to lighten sleep [D30]. LaBerge notes the Tibetan association of the throat with the regulation of sleep and waking as "intriguing" but untested [D1].

The popular Western summary of the stages as "recognising, transforming, multiplying, dissolving" is a paraphrase; the lists above are what was verified [D30].

## Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche: Bön dream yoga

From The Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep (1998; revised 2022) [D28]. Four foundational daytime practices:

1. Changing karmic traces: throughout the day think "I am awake in a dream" or "This is a dream" and become fully present; check that presence actually increases rather than repeating the phrase mechanically; imagine yourself as a dream figure "made of light" [D28].
2. Removing grasping and aversion: when a reaction arises, label it "This anger is a dream. This desire is a dream" and feel it loosen [D28].
3. Strengthening intention, before sleep: review the day, recognising each memory as a dream, then "develop the strongest intention possible to know directly and vividly, while dreaming, that you are dreaming", and pray for a clear dream [D28].
4. Cultivating memory and joyful effort, on waking: review the night; record dreams; rejoice if lucid, strengthen determination if not; commit to consistency [D28].

Preparation for the night includes the "Nine Breathings of Purification", an alternate-nostril clearing of the channels, and guru yoga. The main practice divides the night into four sessions roughly two hours apart, each with a posture, breathing, chakra and visualisation; the throat chakra is the focus of the first session [D28]. The four-session structure and the foundational practices were verified against Wangyal's own articles; the exact visualisations of each session should be read in the book [D28].

## Chögyal Namkhai Norbu: the practice of natural light

From Dream Yoga and the Practice of Natural Light (1992; revised 2002) [D29]. Lying in bed, relaxed, with a little deep breathing if agitated, "concentrate on a white Tibetan syllable [A] at the center of your body" at heart level; an English A is acceptable. The A should be luminous and sharp at first, then "relax slowly but completely" while keeping its presence; repeat if you do not fall asleep. If concentration is hard, write an A on paper and stare at it, or visualise a chain of A's rising from the heart to the crown and descending. If you tend to fall asleep immediately, use an A that is not very bright; "if dreams still do not come, concentrate on the throat chakra, and visualize a red A"; a red ball will do. Put a picture of a white A near the bed as a reminder. On waking, sound the A [D29].

Norbu states that practitioners should use only the instructions received from their own master [D29]. The Aro gTér teaching of a grapefruit-sized white A at the throat within a five-coloured sphere, with women lying on the right side and men on the left, is a related but distinct lineage instruction [D36].

## Where these meet Western methods

Wangyal's bedtime review and strong intention are, in secular terms, pre-sleep dream-sign review and MILD's "remember to remember" [D1][D28]. LaBerge adapted the Tibetan throat visualisations as wake-initiated techniques: the white dot, the black dot, and the dream lotus and flame from Tarthang Tulku [D1]. He also distilled the "power of resolution": by day think that "all things are of the substance of dreams"; at night "firmly resolve" to comprehend the dream state, with the Tibetan note that it takes "no fewer than twenty-one efforts each morning" [D1][D35].

## Evidence level

Tradition. The one traditional claim LaBerge tested, that sleeping on the right side favours lucidity, is described in his 2003 chapter [A13]. Nothing else here has been evaluated in a controlled study.
