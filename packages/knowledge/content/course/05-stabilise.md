## What to do

Expect to become lucid, and have a plan for when you do. Novices "often wake up the moment they become lucid" [D1].

- Stay calm. Restrain the urge to jump and shout; the Course compares it to being "dealt a winning hand in poker" [D5].
- Rub your hands together and feel it, while saying "this is a lucid dream". This was the standard stabiliser in Aspy's study [D11].
- Look around at details and keep your eyes moving. Fixing your gaze on one point blurs the scene and can end the dream within seconds [D7][D8].
- Engage the senses: listen, touch things, talk [D1]. Repeating "This is a dream" or "I'm dreaming" quietly reminds you where you are [D1]; calling out "clarity" is a community habit with no known source.
- When the dream fades (colour and detail go first), spin like a top and feel it, telling yourself the next thing you see will probably be a dream. Then test your state where you land [D1].

## Why

All of these work the same way: they load the senses with the dream, so attention cannot shift to the body in bed [D1]. LaBerge used spinning in 40 of 100 lucid dreams; it produced a new dream scene 85 percent of the time, and lucidity lasted in 97 percent of those [D1]. Expectation shapes the outcome: he could only ever spin into his bedroom, because that is what he expected [D1]. The other edge is too much involvement, which makes you forget you are dreaming; the Course has you review lost lucid dreams for what came just before [D5].

## Today's practice

- Rehearsed the first-seconds routine (calm, rub hands, look around) while awake
- Practised spinning in your mind, feeling it as vividly as you can
- Read back over one lucid or nearly lucid dream for where it slipped

## Ready to move on

After 2 more lucid dreams, or after 4 weeks. You can move on sooner whenever you like.
