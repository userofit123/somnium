## What to do

Write in the journal every morning, even when nothing comes back. A morning with "no recall" still counts: it keeps the habit and the log honest [D20].

When you wake, keep still and ask "What was I dreaming?" before anything else. LaBerge: "Don't move from the position in which you awaken, as any body movement may make your dream harder to remember. Also, don't think of the day's concerns" [C1]. If nothing comes, ask "What was I just thinking?" and "How was I just feeling?", then rebuild the dream backwards, scene by scene [C1].

Before sleep, set the intention: tell yourself "I will remember my dreams", or write it in the journal [C1][C2]. Write at least a few key words the moment you wake, then fill in the rest later [C1].

Where you can, sleep a little later rather than going to bed earlier: dream periods grow longer towards morning, so the last hour of sleep holds the longest dreams [C1][C2].

## Why

Recall is the gate. Lucid-dream frequency goes with dream-recall frequency (r = .57 in a representative German sample) [B33], and in the international induction study better recall predicted who succeeded [B38]. Keeping a logbook raises recall, most of all in people whose recall starts low [B48][C30]. LaBerge puts it plainly: "As you record more dreams, you will remember more" [C1]. Every training programme makes recall the first step; the Lucidity Institute's course does not start induction until recall is steady [D5][D1].

## Today's practice

- Wrote in the journal this morning (a dream or "no recall")
- Lay still and asked "What was I dreaming?" before moving
- Set the intention "I will remember my dreams" before sleep

## Ready to move on

When you have recalled 4 or more dreams in the last 7 nights. You can move on sooner whenever you like.
