## What to do

Pick two checks. The defaults are pinching your nose and trying to breathe through it, and reading some text, looking away and reading it again. LaBerge: "Don't conclude that you are awake unless you have solid proof (for example, the writing stays the same every time you look at it)" [C1]. Text tends to change on a second look in dreams [C33]. Add the memory test: think back over the last few minutes; if you cannot say how you got here, you may be dreaming [C1]. Tholey calls it "one of the most effective tests" [C4].

Do them at five to ten planned moments a day that resemble your dreams, and whenever something is surprising, unlikely or emotional [C1]. Ask the question as if you really did not know. After satisfying yourself that you are awake, imagine: "Okay, I'm not dreaming, now. But if I were, what would it be like?" [C1]. Use the reminders and "Log a check" so the count is yours to see.

## Why

Be honest about the evidence: reality testing on its own did not raise lucid dreaming in the Australian study [B37], added nothing to MILD in the international study [B38], and was ineffective in most recent studies that included it [B35]. Its value is as a habit: a critical, questioning attitude that Tholey found carries into the dream [D8], and a quick way to catch false awakenings later. The common mistake is the mechanical check, "obviously I'm awake", which trains the same answer in the dream [D1][D11][D20]. Fewer, real checks beat many automatic ones [D12].

## Today's practice

- Did 5 or more real checks today, each with the question
- Did one check at a moment that resembled a dream sign
- After a check, imagined what it would be like if this were a dream

## Ready to move on

After 7 days with 5 or more checks each. You can move on sooner whenever you like.
