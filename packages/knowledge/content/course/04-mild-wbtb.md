## What to do

This is the protocol from the Australian induction study [D11]. Plan it for two or three nights a week, on nights when you can sleep in.

1. Set an alarm for about five hours after you go to bed [D11]. Waking at 4.5 hours gave much lower rates in the lab, so do not go earlier [B40].
2. When it goes off, sit up and recall a dream from just before waking, or any recent one [D11]. Stay up 5 to 10 minutes; longer is fine. In the lab an hour of dream work beat half an hour, but only if the time was spent on dreams: reading fiction gave 9 percent and games 0 percent [B39][D13].
3. Lie down and repeat "The next time I'm dreaming, I will remember that I'm dreaming", meaning the words [D11].
4. At the same time, imagine being back in the dream and noticing something odd, a dream sign, that tells you it is a dream [D11][D1].
5. Repeat until you fall asleep. If your mind wanders, come back to it, so the intention is your last thought [D11].

## Why

MILD with WBTB is the best-supported technique there is [B35]. In the Australian study it raised lucid dreaming from 9.4 to 17.4 percent of nights in one week [B37]; in the international study MILD reached 16.5 percent on average, 18.4 and 20.2 percent in the groups without reality testing and 10.8 to 13.4 percent in the groups with it [B38]. Falling asleep quickly matters: attempts followed by sleep within five minutes succeeded about 46 percent of the time [B37], and within ten minutes was the predictor in the larger study [B38]. Do not rush it, though; the minutes are a correlate, not a target [D12]. LaBerge also teaches MILD at bedtime, and says to let go and just keep the intention if twenty minutes pass without sleep [D5].

## Ready to move on

After your first lucid dream, or after 4 weeks. Then adjust: more WBTB nights if sleep stays good, fewer if it does not.
