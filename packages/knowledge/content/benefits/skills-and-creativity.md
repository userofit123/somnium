Lucid dreamers have long reported rehearsing skills and solving problems in their dreams. A handful of studies have tested the claims. The results are plausible and consistent, and the samples are very small.

## Motor practice

Erlacher and Schredl (2010) assigned 40 participants to lucid practice, physical practice or control on a coin-tossing task; the seven who managed to practise in a lucid dream improved more than controls [B64]. Stumbrys, Erlacher and Schredl (2016) had 68 participants learn a finger-tapping sequence; the lucid, mental and physical practice groups all improved overnight, the control group did not, and sleep quality was unaffected. The authors wrote that lucid practice's effects "seem to be similar to actual PP and MP in wakefulness" [B65][D16]. Schaedlich, Erlacher and Schredl (2017) tested darts in a sleep laboratory: only lucid practisers with few in-dream distractions improved [B66][D17].

The effects are consistent with the mental-practice literature, and the samples of people who actually practised in a dream are in single digits [B64][B66]. Dreamed movement does engage the brain's motor areas: dreamed hand clenching in lucid REM activated the sensorimotor cortex on the opposite side [B19].

## What people actually use lucid dreams for

In an online survey of 301 lucid dreamers, the most common application was simply having fun, followed by nightmare resolution, problem solving, creativity and practising skills [B67].

## Creativity

Lucidity correlates with creativity and need for cognition in cross-sectional work [B82], but no experiment has shown that cultivating lucidity increases waking creativity [B82]. The correlational findings with video-game play and internal locus of control are the same kind of evidence: they describe who tends to be lucid, not what lucidity does [B47][B82].

## Problem solving

The one controlled study of dreaming on a problem did not require lucidity. Barrett's students incubated a self-chosen problem nightly for a week; about two-thirds had dreams addressing it and about one-third reached a solution in a dream, some judged objectively sound [D27]. LaBerge's lucid extension adds the intention to become lucid and pursue the topic consciously [D1]. The incubation article gives both methods.

## Mood

On mood the evidence is modest but positive. A five-week diary study of 149 participants found people felt more refreshed, not less, on mornings after lucid dreams [B68], and the international induction study found slightly higher sleep-quality ratings on lucid nights [B38]. Lucid-dream intensity (control, certainty and positive emotion) is inversely associated with depression, anxiety and stress, whereas frequency alone is not [B71]. One early study found lucid-dream frequency positively correlated with depression in undergraduates, the opposite direction, and direction of cause is unknown either way [B73].

## What to expect

If you want to rehearse a skill, the finger-tapping and darts studies suggest a short, focused rehearsal with few distractions, once you can hold a lucid dream long enough [B65][B66]. If you want ideas, incubation works without lucidity and is the better-evidenced tool [D27]. If you want fun, you are in the majority [B67].
