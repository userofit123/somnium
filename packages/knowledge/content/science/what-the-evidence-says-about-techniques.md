Two systematic reviews and a handful of large studies set the evidence levels used throughout this app. This article summarises them and gives the table.

## The reviews

Stumbrys, Erlacher, Schaedlich and Schredl (2012) reviewed 35 studies. Methodological quality was "relatively low" and "none of the induction techniques were verified to induce lucid dreams reliably and consistently", though some were promising, above all MILD combined with wake-back-to-bed [B34][B39]. Later summaries state that of 17 techniques graded, six had support in at least two studies with no contrary evidence, nine were ambiguous or unreplicated and two were ineffective; the exact list could not be obtained and is reported second-hand [B35].

Tan and Fan (2023) reviewed 19 post-2010 studies covering 14 techniques. They rated MILD the most effective, called SSILD and galantamine promising candidates needing replication, treated wake-back-to-bed as an add-on whose independent effect cannot be separated, and noted that reality testing on its own was ineffective in most studies that included it [B35].

## The large home studies

The National Australian Lucid Dream Induction Study (2017; 169 completers) compared reality testing alone, reality testing with wake-back-to-bed, and both with MILD over one baseline week and one technique week. The full combination raised lucid dreaming from 9.4 percent to 17.4 percent of nights; reality testing alone was least effective; and when participants fell asleep within 5 minutes of finishing MILD, 45.8 percent of those attempts produced a lucid dream [B37][B38].

The International Lucid Dream Induction Study (2020; 355 completers) compared MILD with and without daytime reality testing, SSILD, and a hybrid, all with wake-back-to-bed. Nights with a lucid dream rose from 5.3 percent to 15.8 percent overall. MILD groups without reality testing reached 18.4 and 20.2 percent; SSILD 16.9 percent; the hybrid 13.3 percent; MILD with reality testing was lower. Falling asleep within 10 minutes of the technique gave 18.3 versus 11.1 percent. Better dream recall predicted success, and sleep quality did not suffer [B38].

## The laboratory studies

Erlacher and Stumbrys (2020) woke 51 students from REM after about 6 hours and compared a 60-minute wake period of dream work and MILD (about 53-54 percent (two groups) self-rated lucid dreams, 27 percent signal-verified), 30 minutes of the same (36 percent; 14 percent verified), and 60 minutes of reading (9 percent) or balance games (0 percent) [B39]. A follow-up that moved the interruption to 4.5 hours found much lower rates, suggesting that "the earlier sleep interruption seems to reduce the lucid dream induction rate" [B40].

| MILD with WBTB | Best supported; replicated across labs and large field studies | LaBerge 1980; Aspy 2017, 2020; Erlacher and Stumbrys 2020 [B37][B38][B39] | 17 to 20 percent of nights at home; about 46 percent of attempts if asleep within 5 minutes; about 53-54 percent (two groups) self-rated in a lab nap |
| WBTB alone | Supported as an amplifier, not separable from what is done while awake | Erlacher and Stumbrys 2020; Erlacher 2022 [B39][B40] | 9 percent if reading, 0 percent if gaming; 1 hour better than 30 minutes; 4.5 hours too early |
| SSILD | One large field study | Aspy 2020 [B38] | 16.9 percent of nights |
| Reality testing alone | Weak as a standalone; adds nothing to MILD | Aspy 2017, 2020; Tan and Fan 2023 [B37][B38][B35] | Least effective arm |
| Tholey combined technique | Older, small studies; promising but unreplicated recently | Tholey 1983 [B36] | Median 4 to 5 weeks to first lucid dream (uncontrolled) |
| WILD and DEILD | Lab-documented phenomenon; no controlled training trial | Baird 2019 review [B15] | Not quantified |
| Dream journal and recall training | Prerequisite; raises recall; no direct induction trial | Aspy 2016; Schredl and Erlacher 2011 [B48][B33] | Not applicable |
| Mindfulness training | Negative randomised trial (trait correlation only) | Baird 2019 [B46] | No change after 8-week course |

## The practical translation

An evening-prepared, wake-back-to-bed-timed MILD with a fast return to sleep is the best-supported home protocol, with roughly one lucid dream per five or six attempts in motivated samples [B38]. Reality testing is best framed as a low-cost habit that supports recognition, especially after false awakenings, not as an inducer by itself [B35][B37][B38]. Everything else carries the caveat in the table.
