You cannot become lucid in a dream you do not remember, or at least you cannot know that you did. Recall is the foundation, and it is trainable.

## The baseline

In a representative German sample of 931 people over 14, age, sex and size of home town had small but significant effects: women and big-city residents recalled more, and recall declined with age [C25]. Lucid-dream frequency correlated r = .57 with dream-recall frequency in the companion sample [C26]. In Aspy's international study, participants recalled on average 1.7 dreams per night on ordinary nights and 2.8 on nights with a lucid dream [C33].

## What drives recall

Personality, creativity, nocturnal awakenings and attitude to dreams were all significant in a 444-person model but together explained only 8.4 percent of the variance [C28]. The most robust factor is waking from REM: across 35 laboratory awakening studies, mean recall was 81.9 percent from REM versus 43 percent from non-REM [C29]. How you are woken matters too: alarms reduce recall relative to a spoken name [C29a]. Sleep researchers generally accept that dreams are recalled only when the sleeper wakes directly from them [C1].

## The aids, from LaBerge's chapter and the course

1. Extra sleep. "It is more effective for increasing dream recall to sleep later in the morning than to go to bed earlier at night", because REM periods lengthen later in the night: "The first dream of the night is the shortest, perhaps only ten minutes ... after eight hours of sleep, dream periods can be forty-five minutes to an hour long" [C1][C2].
2. Intention. Write "I will remember my dreams" in the journal before bed; tell yourself you will have interesting dreams [C1][C2].
3. A visible reminder by the bed [C2].
4. Ask the question on waking. "Ask yourself this question the moment you awaken: 'What was I dreaming?' Do this first ... Don't move from the position in which you awaken, as any body movement may make your dream harder to remember. Also, don't think of the day's concerns." If nothing comes, ask "What was I just thinking?" and "How was I just feeling?", then rebuild backwards: "When you recall a scene, ask yourself what happened before that, and before that, reliving the dream in reverse" [C1].
5. An alarm at REM-rich times. "REM periods occur about every 90 minutes ... good times to set an alarm for are 4.5, 6 and 7.5 hours after you go to sleep" [C1][C2].
6. Write immediately. "Don't wait until you get up in the morning ... be sure to write down at least a few key words about the dream immediately upon awakening." Note the time, key events, feelings and "any verbatim speech or text", then expand in the morning [C1][C2].
7. Keep the journal by the bed with a pen, light and clock [C2].

Garfield's advice is similar: recall is a learned skill, and if you wake with nothing, "gently roll into the other sleep position and lie there for a moment" [C42].

## Does journaling itself help?

Schredl (2002) found in 285 participants over two weeks that a diary increased recall in low and medium recallers but decreased it in high recallers [C30]; a 2025 four-week study of 81 participants reported the expected logbook enhancement effect [C30a]. Aspy (2016) showed that single retrospective questions underestimate recall and that keeping a logbook enhances it [B48]. LaBerge's summary: "As you record more dreams, you will remember more" [C1].

Voice memos and keyword anchors are common app features, but no controlled study comparing voice to written capture was found; the "stay still" instruction is likewise LaBerge's clinical advice rather than a trial result [C1].

## How many dreams a night

Sleep cycles run about every 90 minutes, giving four to six cycles with REM lengthening across the night [C31]. "You may have more than one dream during a REM (dream) period, separated by short arousals that are most often forgotten" [C1]. The course's targets: at least one dream per night for seven nights in a row, then aim for two, counting any fragment as one [C2].

## Vitamin B6

A randomised trial found 240 mg of B6 increased the amount of dream content recalled without changing vividness or sleep, but did not increase lucidity, and long-term high doses carry a risk of nerve damage [B55]. The app does not recommend it.

## The app's measure

The app counts dreams per morning from your entries rather than asking you to estimate, keeps a separate retrospective item for comparison with published norms, and warns that the first journal weeks inflate your apparent improvement [B48][C2].
