LaBerge and Rheingold introduce "dreamsigns" as the peculiar, recurring features of your own dreams that can serve as cues to recognise the dreaming state [C1]. The Lucidity Institute's course gives the compact definition: "Dreamsigns are objects or events that are impossible or improbable in waking reality" [C2].

The book's own illustration is a "mutant lens" that kept multiplying in the author's dreams: "I resolved that in future dreams like this I would notice the mutant lens as a dreamsign. And indeed, I have become lucid in at least a dozen dreams by recognizing this particular oddity. Each of us has his or her own individual dreamsigns, though some are familiar to most of us, like the case of going to work in your pajamas" [C1].

## Why bother

The Lucidity Institute reports: "In a NightLight experiment studying the role of dreamsigns in the initiation of lucidity, we found that people were more likely to reach lucidity in a dream if they showed a high awareness of the strangeness of dreamsigns" [C2]. The underlying study is an in-house survey, not a peer-reviewed trial [C2].

## The four categories

LaBerge states that the inventory "lists types of dreamsigns organized according to the way people naturally seem to categorize their experiences in dreams. There are four primary categories" [C1]. The first covers what the dreamer perceives as happening inside themselves; the other three classify elements of the dream environment [C1]. Definitions and examples are from the book [C1].

| Category | Definition | Sub-types | Examples quoted in the book |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inner awareness | "You have a peculiar thought, a strong emotion, feel an unusual sensation, or have altered perceptions." | Thoughts, Emotions, Sensations, Perceptions | "When I found the door locked, I 'wished' it open." / "I am filled with extreme anxiety and remorse." / "I seem to lift 'out of body'." / "Somehow I could see perfectly without my glasses." |
| Action | "You, another dream character, or a dream thing (including inanimate objects and animals) do something unusual or impossible in waking life." ## The quiet ones

Context signs often consist of ordinary elements in odd combination: "Sometimes in dreams the combination of elements - people, places, actions, or things, is odd, although there is nothing inherently strange about any item by itself" [C1]. These are the easiest to miss and, in most journals, the most common. Tholey's first lucid dream came from one: seeing a long-dead aunt [C4].

## Two levels

That gives a two-level taxonomy: four categories and eighteen sub-types. The book's "target" is chosen at category level while the sub-types make the tag list more specific, so the app stores both [C1][C2]. Community guides often abbreviate the categories as IA, A, F and C in the margin of a journal [C46].

## Rating your awareness

The course rates how aware you were of each sign on a 0 to 4 scale: 0, you only noticed it was odd after waking; 1, you noticed during the dream but did not try to explain it; 2, you tried to explain it but did not become lucid; 3, you realised you were dreaming; 4, you were already lucid when it occurred [C2]. The app asks this for each sign you confirm.

## A worked example

The course marks an eight-line swim-practice dream with eight signs: context (at swim practice as if 12 years ago; no swimsuit), action (find one and put it on; a boy hurts me), form (the pool becomes a sea; a waterfall edge like a dam; the boy gets older) and inner awareness (I have a crush on the boy) [C2]. Most dreams, read carefully, carry this many.

## What to do with them

The dream-sign targeting technique explains the cataloguing exercise: collect a dozen dreams, mark the signs, classify, count, target the most frequent category, and look for it in waking life [C1]. The app does the counting; the noticing is yours.
