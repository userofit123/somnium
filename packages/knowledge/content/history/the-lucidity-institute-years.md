Once lucid dreaming could be verified, a small field grew up around it. Most of the institutions, books and devices that people still refer to date from these two decades.

## Lucidity Letter

Jayne Gackenbach began Lucidity Letter in December 1981 as a newsletter mailed to about 40 colleagues. It became a journal in 1985 and was published by the non-profit Lucidity Association from 1987. The full archive is hosted by MacEwan University [A39]. Many of the primary texts quoted in this app, including Tholey's own accounts of his methods, appeared there [A25].

## The Association for the Study of Dreams

The Association for the Study of Dreams was formed in 1983 in the San Francisco Bay Area, briefly named Dream Workers International and then Dream Research Society. The word "International" was officially re-added in 2004, giving the IASD [A40]. The 1970s dreamwork movement around Patricia Garfield and Ann Faraday fed into its founding [A29][A30].

## The first academic collection

Gackenbach and LaBerge edited Conscious Mind, Sleeping Brain (1988), the first academic collection on the topic. It includes Snyder and Gackenbach's review of prevalence, which established the convention of counting "frequent lucid dreamers" as those with one or more lucid dreams a month, and Tholey's chapter on lucidity training as self-healing [A41][D10].

## The standard books

LaBerge's Lucid Dreaming (1985) and, with Howard Rheingold, Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming (1990) remain the standard popular introductions [A42]. The 1990 book is also a workbook: its chapters move from dream recall and dream signs through state testing, MILD and wake-initiated methods to stabilisation, control and applications [D1]. Most of the technique steps in this app are quoted or closely paraphrased from it.

## The Lucidity Institute

LaBerge founded the Lucidity Institute in 1987 to run research and courses. Its newsletter NightLight began in 1989, with Lynne Levitan as a principal collaborator on field studies of MILD, reality testing and cueing [A42][A43]. Levitan and LaBerge's A Course in Lucid Dreaming (1995) is a five-unit workbook, each of the first four units taking a minimum of three weeks, with reading, exercises, record forms and quizzes [D5]. It is not currently sold officially; copies circulate as scanned PDFs [C2].

The NightLight studies produced the nap and wake-back-to-bed findings that still guide timing: an hour of wakefulness before a morning nap made lucidity far more likely than ten minutes [D4].

## DreamLight and NovaDreamer

The DreamLight, a mask that detected REM and flashed light cues, was developed by LaBerge and Levitan in the late 1980s and reported in Lucidity Letter [A42][A43]. Its validation study alternated cue and no-cue nights without the sleeper's knowledge: 11 subjects reported 32 lucid dreams, 22 on cue nights and 10 on non-cue nights [B41]. The cheaper NovaDreamer, designed by Craig Webb, was released in 1993 and discontinued around 2004 [A42][A43]. No current consumer mask has comparable published validation [B41].

## Why these years still matter

The methods, scales and vocabulary of this app, from "dreamsign" to MILD to the nap protocol, were set in this period and have been tested since rather than replaced. The next article picks up the story in 2009, when brain imaging and large field studies arrived.
