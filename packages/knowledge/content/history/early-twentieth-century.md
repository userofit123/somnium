After van Eeden named the lucid dream in 1913, the subject was carried for half a century by a few individual writers rather than by laboratories. Three of them are worth knowing.

## Mary Arnold-Forster (1921)

Mary Arnold-Forster's Studies in Dreams, published in 1921 with a foreword by the psychologist Morton Prince, includes chapters on "Dream Control" and "Flying Dreams". She describes training herself to recognise and alter dreams, including banishing unpleasant ones [A22]. Her book is a practical record by a dreamer rather than a theory, and it is freely available in scanned form [A22].

## Oliver Fox (1920 to 1939)

Hugh Callaway, writing as Oliver Fox, published Astral Projection in 1939, expanding accounts first printed in The Occult Review in 1920 and 1923. Fox called lucid dreams "dreams of knowledge" and framed them in occult terms. That framing kept the work outside academic psychology [A23]. Read past the vocabulary and his descriptions of the state are recognisable.

## Nathan Rapport (1948)

Nathan Rapport, an American psychiatrist, published "Pleasant Dreams!" in Psychiatric Quarterly in 1948, arguing that dreams are best studied by a dreamer who knows that he is dreaming [A24]. It is one of the few mid-century papers on the subject in a medical journal.

## The German school begins

In 1959, the Gestalt psychologist Paul Tholey began the first systematic induction research at the University of Frankfurt. In his own account, "I developed my first technique for inducing lucid dreams in 1959. I called it the 'Reflection Technique'": asking several times a day "Am I awake, or am I dreaming?" to build a critical attitude that carries into sleep [A25]. He had his first lucid dream after four weeks [A25]. Tholey's work has its own article.

## Celia Green (1968)

Celia Green's Lucid Dreams, published in 1968 for the Institute of Psychophysical Research in Oxford, was the first book-length academic survey. Reviewing historical and collected cases, she treated lucid dreams as a distinct class of experience and predicted that they would be found to occur in REM sleep, a prediction confirmed a decade later [A28].

## Why this period matters

These writers established two things that later research confirmed: that ordinary people can train themselves to notice they are dreaming, and that the state has stable features worth describing. What they lacked was a way to prove it to anyone else. That arrived in 1975.
