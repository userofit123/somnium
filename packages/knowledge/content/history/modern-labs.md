The modern era of lucid-dream research begins around 2009, when the field moved from demonstrating that lucidity exists to asking what the lucid brain is doing and which techniques actually work.

## Voss and the "hybrid state"

Ursula Voss, Romain Holzmann, Inka Tuin and J. Allan Hobson reported in 2009 that signal-verified lucid REM sleep showed elevated frontal 40 Hz (gamma) activity, and proposed that lucid dreaming is a hybrid state with features of both waking and dreaming [A44]. The result rests on three participants with usable lucid data, and later work found that frontal scalp gamma can be contaminated by eye-muscle activity [B15]. In 2014 Voss and colleagues reported that frontal electrical stimulation at 25 and 40 Hz during REM increased self-reflective awareness in dreams [A45]. A later attempt at replication found lucidity in both stimulation and sham conditions, so the 2014 result should be described as not consistently replicated [A46].

## Dresler and brain imaging

Martin Dresler and colleagues published the first fMRI contrast of lucid versus non-lucid REM sleep in 2012. Four experienced lucid dreamers were scanned; one produced two signal-verified lucid episodes long enough to analyse, showing activation of precuneus, cuneus, parietal lobules and prefrontal and occipito-temporal cortices [A47]. The review by Benjamin Baird, Sergio Mota-Rolim and Dresler, "The cognitive neuroscience of lucid dreaming" (2019), is the current standard synthesis [A48].

## Erlacher, Stumbrys and the Heidelberg, Mannheim and Bern groups

Daniel Erlacher and Michael Schredl showed in a 2010 pilot study that participants who practised a coin-tossing task in a lucid dream improved more than controls [A53]. The same group produced much of the prevalence data, the standard frequency scales, and the sleep-laboratory wake-back-to-bed studies that fixed the timing advice in this app [A60][B39][B40]. Tadas Stumbrys led the 2012 systematic review that concluded no technique had been shown to induce lucidity reliably on demand, though several looked promising [A49].

## Aspy and the large home studies

Denholm Aspy's National Australian Lucid Dream Induction Study (2017) found reality testing plus wake-back-to-bed plus MILD effective over one week [A50]. His International Lucid Dream Induction Study (2020), with 355 completers, found MILD and the community technique SSILD "similarly effective", each producing lucid dreams on roughly 17 to 20 percent of mornings in the practice week, with no harm to sleep quality [A51]. These are the two studies the technique articles cite most.

## Baird and individual differences

Baird and colleagues found in 2018 that frequent lucid dreamers showed increased resting-state connectivity between the front of the prefrontal cortex and parietal and temporal areas, consistent with a metacognition account of lucidity [B21]. In 2019 Baird's group showed that long-term meditators have more lucid dreams than non-meditators, but that an eight-week mindfulness course did not increase them [B46].

## Konkoly and two-way communication

Karen Konkoly, Ken Paller and co-authors in four laboratories reported "Real-time dialogue between experimenters and dreamers during REM sleep" in 2021. Across 36 participants and 57 REM sessions, 15 sessions contained signal-verified lucidity; of 158 questions posed, 29 were answered correctly [A54]. Konkoly's group went on to pair sound cues with pre-sleep training, first in the laboratory and then in a smartphone app, the only peer-reviewed evidence for any lucid-dreaming app [B42][B43].

## The galantamine trial

LaBerge, LaMarca and Baird's double-blind crossover trial (2018) with 121 participants found that the prescription drug galantamine, taken after a wake-back-to-bed period, substantially increased lucid dream frequency relative to placebo [A52]. The substances article explains why this app still does not recommend it.

## Where the field is now

The honest summary from the current reviews: EEG studies are small and inconsistent, neuroimaging is scant but points to prefrontal and parietal regions, and whether brain stimulation can induce lucidity remains unresolved [B15][B78]. Meanwhile, the best cognitive protocol has been replicated across laboratories and countries, which is why this app teaches it first.
