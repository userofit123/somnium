Until the mid-1970s there was no way to show that a lucid dream happened during sleep at all. Two researchers solved the problem independently, a few years and an ocean apart.

## Keith Hearne, Hull, April 1975

On the morning of 12 April 1975, in the sleep laboratory at the University of Hull, Keith Hearne recorded the lucid dreamer Alan Worsley making a pre-arranged sequence of left-right eye movements during REM sleep, captured on the electro-oculogram [A34][A35]. Because the eyes are not paralysed during REM, this was the first objective signal sent from inside a dream [A34].

Hearne sent his data to Allan Rechtschaffen in Chicago and informed William Dement at Stanford in 1975, presented the work at a conference in Hull in April 1977, and submitted his PhD thesis, Lucid dreams: an electro-physiological and psychological study, to the University of Liverpool in May 1978; it is now openly available [A34][A36]. The thesis was never published as a journal article, and his later outputs appeared in parapsychology and imagery journals, which limited its visibility [A16].

Hearne also patented a "dream machine" that detected REM from breathing and delivered a cue, with UK and US patents granted in 1983 [A34].

## Stephen LaBerge, Stanford, 1978 to 1981

Working independently at Stanford, Stephen LaBerge signalled from his own lucid dream on 13 January 1978, with EEG, eye movements and muscle tone all recorded [A35]. He completed his PhD in psychophysiology at Stanford in 1980 and published "Lucid dreaming as a learnable skill: a case study" the same year [A37]. In 1981, with Lynn Nagel, William Dement and Vincent Zarcone, he published "Lucid dreaming verified by volitional communication during REM sleep", in which five subjects signalled lucidity by eye movements and fist clenches during unequivocal REM sleep [A38].

LaBerge also developed the MILD technique during his doctoral work, and later reported that with it he went from fewer than one lucid dream a month to as many as twenty-six in a month [A37][D1].

## The priority question

Hearne later suggested that summaries he sent to Stanford in 1975 could have reached LaBerge. LaBerge has maintained that his work was independent, and no evidence has been produced that he saw Hearne's material. The two discoveries are now generally treated as parallel, in the manner of Darwin and Wallace, with Hearne first in time and LaBerge first in peer-reviewed print [A35].

Both are credited in the modern literature. The 2016 meta-analysis puts it plainly: "Hearne (1978) and LaBerge (1980) demonstrated that lucid dreams represent an objectively verifiable altered state of consciousness" [A21].

## What the eye signal made possible

Everything in the science section rests on this method. The standard signal became a full-scale left-right-left-right sweep, LRLR, which produces a distinctive trace [B15]. With it, laboratories could time-stamp events inside dreams [B16], contrast lucid with non-lucid REM in brain imaging [B18], and eventually hold a two-way conversation with a sleeper [B27].

## Different afterlives

Hearne's work stayed obscure for decades; LaBerge went on to found the Lucidity Institute, write the standard popular books and build the first cue devices [A42]. The next article covers those years. The difference in their reputations says more about publishing than about the discoveries, which were the same.
