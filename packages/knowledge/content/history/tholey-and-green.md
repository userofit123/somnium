Paul Tholey (1937 to 1998) was a Gestalt psychologist at the Johann Wolfgang Goethe University in Frankfurt. He began the first systematic research on inducing lucid dreams, and much of what later became "reality testing" is his.

## The reflection technique

In his own words: "I developed my first technique for inducing lucid dreams in 1959. I called it the 'Reflection Technique'." The subject asks several times a day "Am I awake, or am I dreaming?" to build a critical attitude that carries into sleep. Tholey had his first lucid dream after four weeks, triggered by seeing a long-dead aunt [A25][C4].

He stressed pairing the question with a real test. His physical test: turn 180 degrees and try to stand still; in a dream "the body continues to turn ... or the surroundings begin to revolve". His mental test: "trying to remember what has happened during the immediately preceding period of time", since "bizarre events or a lapse of memory" may indicate a dream [C4]. Because a memory test fails after a false awakening, he recommended "turning a light on ... upon waking up. If the light does not go on, this may signify a dream state" [C4].

## The combined technique

He later added intention and autosuggestion to produce the "combined technique" (1982 to 1983), published "Techniques for inducing and manipulating lucid dreams" in Perceptual and Motor Skills in 1983, and used the German term Klartraum with explicit criteria for distinguishing lucid from non-lucid dreams [A26][A27]. The 1983 paper was based on "research conducted over several years and with over 200 subjects" [D6].

Tholey claimed that subjects who had never had a lucid dream "will have the first one after a median time of 4 to 5 [weeks]" and that most who persist "experience at least one lucid dream every night" [C1]. This is Tholey's self-report, not a controlled result [C4]. A later independent test, cited in the 2020 international study, found lucid dreams on at most 6 percent of days with his combined technique [D12].

## Dream characters

Tholey's most unusual study asked nine experienced lucid dreamers to test dream characters: to have them draw or write, name unknown words, find rhymes, make verses and solve arithmetic. Some characters agreed and succeeded, "although the arithmetic accomplishments were poor". Tholey concluded that nothing contradicts the assumption that dream characters have "consciousness in a specific sense" [D9].

## Nightmares

His 1988 chapter presents "conciliatory interaction with threatening dream figures" as the most effective principle for nightmares, and reports a preliminary study of 62 students in which confronted figures lost their threatening character [D10]. He found that attacking figures made them regress and could produce anxiety, guilt and "avenging figures" [D8].

## His practical guide

With Kaleb Utecht he wrote Schoepferisch traeumen: Der Klartraum als Lebenshilfe (1987) [A27]. No book or article by Tholey titled "Schein und Wirklichkeit" could be found in his bibliography; that title appears to be a misattribution [A25].

## Celia Green, in parallel

In Oxford, Celia Green's Lucid Dreams (1968) was the first book-length academic survey. Reviewing historical and collected cases, she treated lucid dreams as a distinct class of experience and predicted they would be found in REM sleep [A28]. Where Tholey worked on induction, Green worked on description; the laboratory verification that followed owed something to both.

## What survives of Tholey in this app

The reality-testing exercise is his, with LaBerge's refinements. The memory test and light-switch check are his. The advice to converse with threatening figures rather than fight them is his. And his observation that with practice "active questioning ... ultimately gives rise to a passively receptive focus on current experience" is the idea behind the community technique later called all-day awareness [C4].
