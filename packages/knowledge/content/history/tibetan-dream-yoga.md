The most developed pre-modern practice of lucidity is Tibetan dream yoga, in Tibetan rmi lam, usually written "milam". It forms one of the Six Yogas of Naropa, the eleventh-century Indian master whose teachings were carried into Tibet by Marpa and Milarepa and are central to the Kagyu school [A11]. The Gelug school also transmits them [D30].

## The stages

The traditional progression moves from recognition to transformation to insight. The practitioner first learns to recognise the dream as a dream, then to transform dream objects, then to see through the dream and, by extension, waking appearances, as empty [A7].

Gampopa's stages, as presented in modern scholarship, are: seizing the dream (by day regard all perceptions as dreamlike, at night resolve firmly to recognise dreams); training (within the dream face whatever arises without fear while knowing it is a dream); blessing as illusory and removing fear; and meditating on reality, analysing all dream experience as one's own unborn mind [D30]. Tsongkhapa's four trainings cover the same ground: retaining awareness in dreams; controlling and increasing dream content; overcoming fear and understanding the illusory nature; and meditating on the suchness of dreams [D30].

The popular Western summary of the stages as "recognising, transforming, multiplying, dissolving" is a paraphrase; the lists above are what has been verified [D30].

## The practice

Instructions include lying on the right side in the "lion posture", visualising a lotus at the throat with syllables, and sleeping with a strong resolve to recognise the dream [D30]. The dream yoga text that LaBerge worked from, in Evans-Wentz's 1935 translation, describes "comprehending it by the power of resolution": by day think continuously that "all things are of the substance of dreams", and when about to sleep "firmly resolve" to comprehend the dream state [D35][D1].

## The Bön tradition

The indigenous Bön religion of Tibet has a parallel system, taught in the West chiefly through Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche's The Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep (1998) [A12]. The traditions article describes his four foundational practices.

## Reaching the West

The first scholarly English presentation was W. Y. Evans-Wentz's Tibetan Yoga and Secret Doctrines (Oxford, 1935) [D35]. Stephen LaBerge compared the Tibetan stages with Western induction methods and tested the traditional claim that sleeping on the right side favours lucidity [A13].

## Not the same thing

A 2026 paper in the International Journal of Dream Research cautions that dream yoga and lucid dreaming share a core skill but differ in aim, and should not simply be equated [A14]. Dream yoga is a contemplative path whose stated goal is recognising the dreamlike nature of all experience, not lucid dreaming for its own sake, and most teachers state that the practices are meant to be received from a qualified teacher [D30].
