Popular lucid-dreaming literature carries two stories that have been repeated so often they feel like history. Both have been examined carefully, and both fail. This app does not use them as evidence.

## The Senoi "dream people"

A recurring claim in 1970s dream literature, repeated in Patricia Garfield's bestseller Creative Dreaming (1974), is that the Senoi people of Malaya practised a communal morning dream-sharing and dream-control system that produced a peaceful, psychologically healthy society [A29][A31].

The source is Kilton Stewart (1902 to 1965), who visited the Senoi in 1934. G. William Domhoff's The Mystique of Dreams (1985) and his later online update conclude that Stewart "misunderstood Senoi psychology and dream practices, and incorrectly attributed his own ideas to them", that the Senoi do have a dream theory but do not practise "Senoi Dream Theory as Americans know it", and that there is no good evidence that dream control is achieved by Stewart's methods [A31].

The claim should be presented as a myth [A31]. The "Senoi" advice to confront and conquer dream enemies survives in some nightmare guidance; LaBerge allows the conquest approach only in special cases, and Tholey's research found that attacking dream figures tends to produce avenging figures rather than peace [D1][D8].

## Carlos Castaneda and don Juan

Castaneda's books about the Yaqui sorcerer "don Juan" introduced many readers to "dreaming" as a discipline. The instruction to find one's hands in a dream, given in Journey to Ixtlan (1972) and elaborated in The Art of Dreaming (1993), is still circulated as an induction cue [A32][D25].

Richard de Mille's Castaneda's Journey (1976) and The Don Juan Papers (1980) showed through library records and linguistic evidence that the field work could not have occurred as described. The character of don Juan is now generally regarded as fabricated [A33].

The hands technique may still work as a prospective-memory cue, but it has no documented ethnographic origin [A32]. The dreamer's own body is one of the most stable reference points in a dream, so looking at the hands can steady a scene; that argument comes from Scott Sparrow, quoted by LaBerge, not from any sorcerer [D1][D38].

## Smaller doubts

Several other stories are repeated beyond what sources support:

- Galen is often listed among ancient observers of lucid dreaming, but his only relevant text is about diagnosing illness from dream imagery, and no passage describing lucidity was found [A5].
- Augustine's account of the physician Gennadius is called "the first written report of a lucid dream" on popular sites; it post-dates Aristotle, and at least one scholarly volume suggests Augustine may have invented or embellished it [A6][A8].
- Claims that the Upanishads contain "the first mention of lucid dreaming, around 1000 BC" are not supported by a specific passage [A9].
- Specific figures for Hervey de Saint-Denys (1,946 nights, a first lucid dream on the 207th night, "22 volumes") could not be checked against his text [A17][C5].
- In September 2024 the company REMspace issued press releases claiming the first "two-way communication" between two sleeping people in lucid dreams, followed by claims of controlling a car from a dream. These results have not been peer-reviewed and should be labelled as unverified marketing [A57].

## Why it matters

A technique can work for reasons that have nothing to do with the story attached to it. Looking at your hands may help you become lucid; the Senoi may have interesting dream customs. But the app's job is to tell you what is known, and part of that is saying which familiar stories are not.
