Reports of knowing that you are dreaming go back more than two thousand years. Some of the names repeated in popular histories hold up; some do not.

## Aristotle (4th century BCE)

The earliest surviving Western description is in Aristotle's short treatise On Dreams. He writes that "often, when one is asleep, there is something in consciousness which declares that what then presents itself is but a dream" [A3]. He uses the observation as an analogy: just as noticing a finger pressed under the eyeball stops you believing that one object is two, noticing that you are asleep stops you believing the dream image [A3]. Scholars describe him as "probably the first" to record the phenomenon, rather than the first ever [A4].

## Galen: not a source

Galen is often listed alongside Aristotle, but the only relevant text, On Diagnosis from Dreams, concerns using dream imagery to diagnose bodily humours, not awareness within sleep. Its authorship is itself disputed [A5]. No passage describing lucidity was found, so Galen should not be cited as a lucid-dreaming source.

## Augustine of Hippo (415 CE)

In Letter 159, Augustine recounts a dream told to him by Gennadius, a physician of Carthage. A youth appears to Gennadius on two nights; on the second he asks where Gennadius saw him before ("in sleep"), where his body is now ("in my bed"), and then tells him "even now you are seeing in sleep" [A6]. Augustine uses the story as an argument for the soul's survival after death, treating it as "a kind of preview of the afterlife" [A7]. Popular sites call it "the first written report of a lucid dream", which overstates matters: it post-dates Aristotle, and at least one scholarly volume suggests Augustine may have invented or embellished it [A8].

## India: the Mandukya Upanishad

The Mandukya Upanishad analyses the syllable Om into four states of the self: waking, dreaming (taijasa, "the luminous one"), deep sleep and the fourth, turiya, pure awareness [A9][A10]. This is a theory of states of consciousness rather than a description of lucid dreaming, but it matters because later Indian and Tibetan practices treat the dream state as a field for cultivating awareness [A9]. Claims that the Upanishads contain "the first mention of lucid dreaming, around 1000 BC" are not supported by a specific passage and should be treated as unverified [A9].

## Tibet: dream yoga

The most developed pre-modern practice of lucidity is Tibetan dream yoga, one of the Six Yogas of Naropa, the eleventh-century Indian master whose teachings were carried into Tibet and became central to the Kagyu school [A11]. It has its own article in this section.

## Islam: Ibn al-Arabi (1165 to 1240)

The Andalusian Sufi master Ibn al-Arabi classified dreams into three kinds and, according to the secondary literature, held that "a person must control his thoughts in a dream" [A7]. This quotation reaches modern reviews through later scholarship; no primary Arabic passage was located, so the attribution should be presented as reported rather than quoted directly [A7].

## Thomas Aquinas (13th century)

In the Summa Theologiae, discussing whether sleep hinders judgment, Aquinas writes that "sometimes while asleep a man may judge that what he sees is a dream, discerning, as it were, between things and their images", and that orderly thought in sleep happens "especially towards the end of sleep in sober men and those who are gifted with a strong imagination" [A15]. The Psi Encyclopedia notes that this correctly anticipates the modern finding that lucidity clusters in late-night REM sleep and in imaginative people [A16].

## What to take from this

The ancient observers agree on the core fact: a sleeper can sometimes know they are asleep. None of them trained it systematically, with the exception of the Tibetan tradition. Systematic training waits for the nineteenth century and a French aristocrat with a very long diary.
