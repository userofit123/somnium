The Dutch psychiatrist and novelist Frederik van Eeden presented "A Study of Dreams" to the Society for Psychical Research in London; it was printed in the Society's Proceedings in 1913 [A19]. It is the paper that gave the experience its name.

## Nine kinds of dream

Van Eeden classified his own dreams into nine types. "The seventh type of dreams, which I called lucid dreams," he wrote, reporting "352 cases in the period between January 20, 1898, and December 26, 1912" [A19]. He recorded the dreams he considered most important rather than every dream, and 352 of those recorded are classed as lucid [C6][C6a].

## Experiments inside the dream

What makes the paper still readable is the experiments. He describes trying to shatter a claret glass with his fist and finding that it broke only after a delay. He notes the double awareness that "the physical body is sleeping and has quite a different position" from the dream body [A19][A20]. He treated the lucid dream as a laboratory and himself as both experimenter and subject, which is how the field has worked ever since.

## A strict definition

Van Eeden's definition required more than awareness. It required full memory of waking life and the ability to act voluntarily. Later researchers, notably Green and McCreery in 1994, treat awareness alone as sufficient, and this difference still affects prevalence estimates: a survey using van Eeden's strict definition would count fewer lucid dreamers than one using the modern definition [A21].

## Where it was published

The paper appeared in the proceedings of a psychical research society rather than a psychology journal, and that placement, like Oliver Fox's framing of lucid dreams as "astral projection" a generation later, kept the subject at the margins of academic psychology for decades [A23]. It was not until Celia Green's 1968 survey and the laboratory work of the 1970s that the experience was treated as a normal object of science [A28].

## Reading him today

The full text of "A Study of Dreams" is available online [A19]. The experiences section of this app quotes short passages from it, with attribution, because few later accounts describe the state as carefully as the man who named it.
