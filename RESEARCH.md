# Part A: History, origins and prevalence


A lucid dream is a dream in which the dreamer knows, while the dream is still going on, that he or she is dreaming [1][2]. The word "lucid" for this state is barely a century old, but reports of the experience go back more than two thousand years, and formal practices for cultivating it are at least a thousand years old. This section traces that history, flags claims that are doubtful or debunked, and summarises what is known about how common lucid dreaming is.

### 1. Antiquity and the Middle Ages

#### Aristotle (4th century BCE)

The earliest surviving Western description is in Aristotle's short treatise *On Dreams* (*De Insomniis*, part of the *Parva Naturalia*). In the third section (Bekker 462a) he writes that "often, when one is asleep, there is something in consciousness which declares that what then presents itself is but a dream" (J. I. Beare's translation) [3]. He uses the observation as an analogy: just as noticing a finger pressed under the eyeball stops you believing that one object is two, noticing that you are asleep stops you believing the dream image. Scholars describe him as "probably the first" to record the phenomenon, rather than the first ever [4].

#### Galen (2nd century CE)

Galen is often listed alongside Aristotle in popular histories, but the only relevant text, *On Diagnosis from Dreams* (*De dignotione ex insomniis*), concerns using dream imagery to diagnose bodily humours, not awareness within sleep. Its authorship is itself disputed; it may be a later excerpt of Galenic doctrine [5]. No passage describing lucidity was found, so Galen should not be cited as a lucid-dreaming source.

#### Augustine of Hippo (415 CE)

In Letter 159, written in 415 CE to his friend Evodius, Augustine recounts a dream told to him by Gennadius, a physician of Carthage. A youth appears to Gennadius on two nights; on the second he asks where Gennadius saw him before ("in sleep"), where his body is now ("in my bed"), and then tells him "even now you are seeing in sleep" [6]. Augustine uses it as an argument for the soul's survival after death; the Frontiers review by Mota-Rolim and colleagues describes him as treating it as "a kind of preview of the afterlife" [7]. Popular sites call it "the first written report of a lucid dream", which overstates matters: it post-dates Aristotle, and at least one recent scholarly workshop volume suggests Augustine may have invented or embellished the story [8].

#### India: the Mandukya Upanishad

The *Mandukya Upanishad* (first millennium BCE, exact date uncertain) analyses the syllable Om into four states of the self: waking (vaishvanara), dreaming (taijasa, "the luminous one"), deep sleep (prajna) and the fourth, turiya, pure awareness [9][10]. This is a theory of states of consciousness rather than a description of lucid dreaming; it is important to the history because later Indian and Tibetan practices treat the dream state as a field for cultivating awareness. Claims on some websites that the Upanishads contain "the first mention of lucid dreaming, around 1000 BC" are not supported by a specific passage and should be treated as unverified.

#### Tibet: dream yoga (milam) and the Bön tradition

The most developed pre-modern practice of lucidity is Tibetan dream yoga (Tibetan *rmi lam*, usually written "milam"). It forms one of the Six Yogas of Naropa, the eleventh-century Indian master whose teachings were carried into Tibet by Marpa and Milarepa and are central to the Kagyu school [11]. The practitioner first learns to recognise the dream as a dream, then to transform dream objects, then to see through the dream and, by extension, waking appearances, as empty [7]. The indigenous Bön religion of Tibet has a parallel system, taught in the West chiefly through Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche's *The Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep* (1998) [12]. The first scholarly English presentation was W. Y. Evans-Wentz's *Tibetan Yoga and Secret Doctrines* (Oxford, 1935). Stephen LaBerge compared the Tibetan stages with Western induction methods and tested the traditional claim that sleeping on the right side favours lucidity [13]. A 2026 paper in the *International Journal of Dream Research* cautions that dream yoga and lucid dreaming share a core skill but differ in aim, and should not simply be equated [14].

#### Islam: Ibn al-Arabi (1165-1240)

The Andalusian Sufi master Ibn al-Arabi classified dreams into three kinds and, according to the secondary literature, held that "a person must control his thoughts in a dream" [7]. This quotation reaches modern reviews through Gackenbach and LaBerge's 1988 volume and Kelly Bulkeley's work; no primary Arabic passage was located for this document, so the attribution should be presented as "reported by" rather than quoted directly from Ibn al-Arabi.

#### Thomas Aquinas (13th century)

In the *Summa Theologiae* (I, q. 84, a. 8), discussing whether the suspension of the senses in sleep hinders judgment, Aquinas writes that when the sleep-inducing "evaporation" is slight, "sometimes while asleep a man may judge that what he sees is a dream, discerning, as it were, between things and their images", and that orderly thought in sleep happens "especially towards the end of sleep in sober men and those who are gifted with a strong imagination" [15]. The Psi Encyclopedia notes that this correctly anticipates the modern finding that lucidity clusters in late-night REM sleep and in imaginative people [16].

### 2. The nineteenth century: Hervey de Saint-Denys

Marie-Jean-Leon, Marquis d'Hervey de Saint-Denys (1822-1892), a French sinologist, is the first person known to have kept a sustained dream journal and used it deliberately to train dream awareness. He began recording his dreams at thirteen or fourteen and in 1867 published, anonymously, *Les reves et les moyens de les diriger: observations pratiques* ("Dreams and the ways to direct them"). Alfred Maury identified him as the author in a footnote to the 1878 edition of *Le sommeil et les reves* [17]. The book argues from his own records that attention to dream recall, together with associative cues, allows the dreamer to become aware inside the dream and to steer it; he used the phrase "reve lucide" roughly fifty years before van Eeden, though in a looser sense [18]. Frequently repeated figures (1,946 nights of recorded dreams, first lucid dream on the 207th night) come from a single encyclopaedia entry and could not be checked against the French text, so they are listed here as unverified. An abridged English translation edited by Morton Schatzman appeared in 1982 (Duckworth), and a complete translation by Carolus den Blanken and Eli Meijer in 2016 [17].

### 3. 1913 to the 1960s: naming and describing the phenomenon

#### Frederik van Eeden coins "lucid dream" (1913)

The Dutch psychiatrist and novelist Frederik van Eeden presented "A Study of Dreams" to the Society for Psychical Research; it was printed in the *Proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research*, vol. 26 (1913), pp. 431-461 [19]. Classifying his own dreams into nine types, he wrote: "The seventh type of dreams, which I called lucid dreams," and reported "352 cases in the period between January 20, 1898, and December 26, 1912." He describes experiments inside the dream, for example trying to shatter a claret glass with his fist and finding that it broke only after a delay, and notes the double awareness that "the physical body is sleeping and has quite a different position" [19][20]. Van Eeden's definition required full memory of waking life and the ability to act voluntarily; later researchers (Green and McCreery, 1994) treat awareness alone as sufficient, and this difference still affects prevalence estimates [21].

#### Early twentieth-century English writers

- Mary Arnold-Forster, *Studies in Dreams* (Allen and Unwin / Macmillan, 1921), with a foreword by Morton Prince, includes chapters on "Dream Control" and "Flying Dreams" and describes training herself to recognise and alter dreams, including banishing unpleasant ones [22].
- Hugh Callaway, writing as Oliver Fox, published *Astral Projection* in 1939 (Rider), expanding accounts first printed in *The Occult Review* in 1920 and 1923. Fox called lucid dreams "dreams of knowledge" and framed them in occult terms, which kept the work outside academic psychology [23].
- Nathan Rapport, an American psychiatrist, published "Pleasant Dreams!" in *Psychiatric Quarterly* 22 (1948), pp. 309-317, arguing that dreams are best studied by a dreamer who knows that he is dreaming [24].

#### Paul Tholey and the German school (1959 onward)

The Gestalt psychologist Paul Tholey (1937-1998) began the first systematic induction research at the Johann Wolfgang Goethe University, Frankfurt. In his own account, "I developed my first technique for inducing lucid dreams in 1959. I called it the 'Reflection Technique'": the subject asks several times a day "Am I awake, or am I dreaming?" to build a critical attitude that carries into sleep; he had his first lucid dream after four weeks [25]. He later added intention and auto-suggestion to produce the "combined technique" (1982-1983), published "Techniques for inducing and manipulating lucid dreams" in *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 57 (1983), pp. 79-90, and used the German term *Klartraum* with explicit criteria for distinguishing lucid from non-lucid dreams [26][27]. His practical guide, with Kaleb Utecht, is *Schoepferisch traeumen: Der Klartraum als Lebenshilfe* (Falken, 1987). No book or article by Tholey titled "Schein und Wirklichkeit" could be found in his bibliography or in library searches; that title appears to be a misattribution.

#### Celia Green (1968)

Celia Green's *Lucid Dreams* (Hamish Hamilton for the Institute of Psychophysical Research, Oxford, 1968) was the first book-length academic survey of the phenomenon. Reviewing historical and collected cases, she treated lucid dreams as a distinct class of experience and predicted that they would be found to occur in REM sleep, a prediction confirmed a decade later [28].

### 4. The 1970s: popular interest, and two debunked influences

#### Patricia Garfield, Ann Faraday and the dreamwork movement

Patricia Garfield's *Creative Dreaming* (1974) was a bestseller that brought dream control, including lucidity, to a wide audience [29]. Ann Faraday, a British psychologist whose PhD at University College London was an experimental study of dreams, published *Dream Power* (1972) and *The Dream Game* (1974, UK 1975), which popularised the dream diary as a self-help tool [30]. Both authors were part of the 1970s "dreamwork" movement that fed into the founding of the Association for the Study of Dreams.

#### The Senoi "dream people": debunked

A recurring claim in 1970s dream literature, repeated in *Creative Dreaming*, is that the Senoi of Malaya practised a communal morning dream-sharing and dream-control system that produced a peaceful, psychologically healthy society. The source is Kilton Stewart (1902-1965), who visited the Senoi in 1934. G. William Domhoff's *The Mystique of Dreams* (University of California Press, 1985) and his later online update conclude that Stewart "misunderstood Senoi psychology and dream practices, and incorrectly attributed his own ideas to them", that the Senoi do have a dream theory but do not practise "Senoi Dream Theory as Americans know it", and that there is no good evidence that dream control is achieved by Stewart's methods [31]. The claim should be presented as a myth.

#### Carlos Castaneda: fiction presented as ethnography

Castaneda's books about the Yaqui sorcerer "don Juan" introduced many readers to "dreaming" as a discipline, and the instruction to find one's hands in a dream, elaborated in *The Art of Dreaming* (1993), is still circulated as an induction cue [32]. Richard de Mille's *Castaneda's Journey* (1976) and *The Don Juan Papers* (1980) showed through library records and linguistic evidence that the field work could not have occurred as described; the character of don Juan is now generally regarded as fabricated [33]. The hands technique may still work as a prospective-memory cue, but it has no documented ethnographic origin.

### 5. Scientific verification: Hearne and LaBerge

#### Keith Hearne, Hull, April 1975

On the morning of 12 April 1975, in the sleep laboratory at the University of Hull, Keith Hearne recorded the lucid dreamer Alan Worsley making a pre-arranged sequence of left-right eye movements during REM sleep, captured on the electro-oculogram [34][35]. Because the eyes are not paralysed during REM, this was the first objective signal sent from inside a dream. Hearne sent his data to Allan Rechtschaffen in Chicago and informed William Dement at Stanford in 1975, presented the work at a Hull conference in April 1977, and submitted his PhD thesis, *Lucid dreams: an electro-physiological and psychological study*, to the University of Liverpool in May 1978 (now openly available, DOI 10.17638/03174691) [34][36]. The thesis was not published as a journal article, and his later outputs appeared in parapsychology and imagery journals, which limited its visibility [16]. Hearne also patented a "dream machine" that detected REM from breathing and delivered a cue (UK patent 2039741, 1983; US patent 4420001, 1983) [34].

#### Stephen LaBerge, Stanford, 1978-1981

Working independently at Stanford, Stephen LaBerge signalled from his own lucid dream on 13 January 1978 with EEG, EOG and EMG all recorded [35]. He completed his PhD in psychophysiology at Stanford in 1980, published "Lucid dreaming as a learnable skill: a case study" (*Perceptual and Motor Skills* 51, 1980, pp. 1039-1042), and in 1981, with Lynn Nagel, William Dement and Vincent Zarcone, "Lucid dreaming verified by volitional communication during REM sleep" (*Perceptual and Motor Skills* 52(3), pp. 727-732), in which five subjects signalled lucidity by eye movements and fist clenches during unequivocal REM sleep [37][38]. LaBerge also developed the MILD technique (Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams) during his doctoral work.

#### The priority question

Hearne later suggested that summaries he sent to Stanford in 1975 could have reached LaBerge; LaBerge has maintained that his work was independent, and no evidence has been produced that he saw Hearne's material. The two discoveries are now generally treated as parallel, in the manner of Darwin and Wallace, with Hearne first in time and LaBerge first in peer-reviewed print [35]. Both are credited in the modern literature, for example Saunders et al. (2016): "Hearne (1978) and LaBerge (1980) demonstrated that lucid dreams represent an objectively verifiable altered state of consciousness" [21].

### 6. Institutions, journals and devices (1981-2000)

- *Lucidity Letter*: begun by Jayne Gackenbach in December 1981 as a newsletter mailed to about 40 colleagues, it became a journal in 1985 and was published by the non-profit Lucidity Association from 1987; the full archive is hosted by MacEwan University [39].
- The Association for the Study of Dreams was formed in 1983 in the San Francisco Bay Area (briefly named Dream Workers International, then Dream Research Society); the word "International" was officially re-added in 2004, giving the IASD [40].
- Gackenbach and LaBerge edited *Conscious Mind, Sleeping Brain* (Plenum, 1988), the first academic collection on the topic; it includes Snyder and Gackenbach's review of prevalence, discussed below [41].
- LaBerge's books *Lucid Dreaming* (Tarcher, 1985) and, with Howard Rheingold, *Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming* (Ballantine, 1990) remain the standard popular introductions [42].
- LaBerge founded the Lucidity Institute in 1987 to run research and courses; its newsletter *NightLight* began in 1989, with Lynne Levitan as a principal collaborator on field studies of MILD, reality testing and cueing [42][43].
- The DreamLight, a mask that detected REM and flashed light cues, was developed by LaBerge and Levitan in the late 1980s and reported in *Lucidity Letter*; the cheaper NovaDreamer, designed by Craig Webb, was released in 1993 and discontinued around 2004 [42][43].

### 7. The modern era (2009 to the present)

#### Brain state and neuroimaging

Ursula Voss, Romain Holzmann, Inka Tuin and J. Allan Hobson, "Lucid dreaming: a state of consciousness with features of both waking and non-lucid dreaming" (*Sleep* 32(9), 2009, pp. 1191-1200), reported elevated frontal 40 Hz (gamma) activity during signal-verified lucid REM sleep and proposed that lucid dreaming is a hybrid state [44]. In 2014 Voss and colleagues reported in *Nature Neuroscience* that frontotemporal transcranial alternating current stimulation at 25 and 40 Hz during REM sleep increased self-reflective awareness in dreams [45]. A later attempt at signal-verified induction found lucidity in both stimulation and sham conditions and did not support the claim that 40 Hz stimulation heightens dream self-awareness, so the 2014 result should be described as not consistently replicated [46].

Martin Dresler and colleagues published the first fMRI contrast of lucid versus non-lucid REM sleep (*Sleep* 35(7), 2012, pp. 1017-1020). Four experienced lucid dreamers were scanned; one produced two signal-verified lucid episodes long enough to analyse, showing activation of precuneus, cuneus, parietal lobules and prefrontal and occipito-temporal cortices [47]. Benjamin Baird, Sergio Mota-Rolim and Dresler's review "The cognitive neuroscience of lucid dreaming" (*Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews* 100, 2019, pp. 305-323) is the current standard synthesis [48].

#### Induction research

Tadas Stumbrys, Daniel Erlacher, Melanie Schaedlich and Michael Schredl, "Induction of lucid dreams: a systematic review of evidence" (*Consciousness and Cognition* 21, 2012, pp. 1456-1475), reviewed 35 studies and concluded that no technique induced lucidity reliably on demand, though several (MILD, Tholey's combined technique, reality testing with wake-back-to-bed, light cues) looked promising [49]. Denholm Aspy's National Australian Lucid Dream Induction Study (*Dreaming* 27(3), 2017, pp. 206-231) found reality testing plus wake-back-to-bed plus MILD effective over one week [50]. His International Lucid Dream Induction Study (*Frontiers in Psychology* 11:1746, 2020; published under the name D. J. Adventure-Heart), with 355 completers, found MILD and SSILD "similarly effective": MILD with wake-back-to-bed yielded lucid dreams on 18-20 percent of mornings in the practice week, SSILD on 17 percent, and the hybrid showed no advantage, with no harm to sleep quality [51]. LaBerge, LaMarca and Baird's double-blind crossover trial (*PLOS ONE* 13(8): e0201246, 2018) with 121 participants found that 4 mg and 8 mg of the cholinesterase inhibitor galantamine, taken after a wake-back-to-bed period, substantially increased lucid dream frequency relative to placebo [52].

#### Motor practice and sport (Heidelberg and Mannheim, Bern)

Daniel Erlacher and Michael Schredl showed in a pilot study (*The Sport Psychologist* 24(2), 2010, pp. 157-167) that participants who practised a coin-tossing task in a lucid dream improved more than controls; Stumbrys, Erlacher and Schredl later compared lucid-dream, physical and mental practice on a finger-tapping task [53]. The same group produced much of the prevalence data below.

#### Two-way communication (2021)

Karen Konkoly, Ken Paller and co-authors in four laboratories (Northwestern University, USA; Osnabrueck University with the Institute of Sleep and Dream Technologies, Germany; Paris Brain Institute, Sorbonne, France; Donders Institute, Radboud University, Netherlands) reported "Real-time dialogue between experimenters and dreamers during REM sleep" (*Current Biology* 31(7), 2021, pp. 1417-1427). Across 36 participants and 57 REM sessions, 15 sessions (26 percent) contained signal-verified lucidity; of 158 questions posed (arithmetic, yes/no, counting stimuli), 29 (18.4 percent) were answered correctly by eye or facial-muscle signals, 5 incorrectly, 28 ambiguously and 96 without response [54].

#### Consumer devices and commercial claims

The Remee, a timer-based LED mask with no REM sensing, raised over half a million dollars on Kickstarter in 2012; the Aurora headband by iWinks, which used EEG, raised about 239,000 dollars in late 2013, shipped late and was later abandoned when its app was withdrawn [55]. A 2019 review in *Frontiers in Neuroscience* concluded that portable induction devices were of unproven reliability [56]. In September 2024 the company REMspace, led by Michael Raduga, issued press releases claiming the first "two-way communication" between two sleeping people in lucid dreams, followed by claims of controlling a car from a dream. These results have not been peer-reviewed; Konkoly has publicly criticised the company for releasing "unfinished, undocumented projects inaccurately represented as breakthroughs", and Raduga's 2023 self-implanted brain electrode is widely noted as a safety concern. These claims should be labelled as unverified marketing [57].

#### Online communities and SSILD

LD4all was announced by its founder pasQuale on 25 May 1996 in the Usenet group alt.dreams.lucid and added a discussion forum in March 1999; DreamViews dates from around 2003; the subreddit r/LucidDreaming was created in 2009 and passed 100,000 subscribers by 2014 (the last two dates rest on weaker sources) [58]. The SSILD technique (Senses Initiated Lucid Dream) was created in 2011 by a user known as CosmicIron on a Chinese lucid-dreaming forum and posted in English on LD4all and DreamViews about a year later; it went from forum technique to a peer-reviewed arm of ILDIS in 2020, where it matched MILD [51][59]. A specific Chinese forum name ("Too Yin") appearing in some accounts could not be confirmed.

### 8. Prevalence and epidemiology

#### Definitions

Since Snyder and Gackenbach's 1988 review in *Conscious Mind, Sleeping Brain*, surveys distinguish lifetime prevalence (at least one lucid dream ever) from "frequent lucid dreamers" (one or more per month). Their narrative review gave "conservative" estimates of 58 percent and 21 percent respectively [21][41]. Most modern questionnaires, including the widely used eight-point frequency scale of Schredl and Erlacher, give respondents a short definition ("being aware that one is dreaming while dreaming") and sometimes an example; unclear definitions can mislead up to a fifth of a sample [21].

#### Meta-analysis: Saunders et al. (2016)

David Saunders, Chris Roe, Graham Smith and Helen Clegg pooled 34 studies from 1966 to 2016 with a total of 24,282 respondents (student, representative, child and interest-group samples) in a quality-effects meta-analysis (*Consciousness and Cognition* 43, pp. 197-215). Lifetime prevalence was 55 percent (95 percent CI 49-62) and 23 percent (CI 20-25) reported lucid dreams once a month or more. Online samples ran higher (65 percent), and heterogeneity was large for prevalence but modest for the frequent-dreamer estimate. The authors note that frequent lucid dreaming is about twice as common as left-handedness [21].

#### Representative samples: Germany

Schredl and Erlacher surveyed a representative German sample (N = 919) and found that 51 percent had experienced a lucid dream at least once and about 20 percent had them monthly or more (*Perceptual and Motor Skills* 112(1), 2011, pp. 104-108). Women reported more lucid dreams than men, frequency declined with age, and lucid dream frequency correlated strongly with dream recall frequency (r = .57) [60].

#### Age and children

Voss, Frenzel, Koppehele-Gossel and Hobson surveyed students aged 6-19 (*Journal of Sleep Research* 21(6), 2012, pp. 634-642) and found lucid dreaming "quite pronounced in young children" with incidence dropping sharply at about age 16, which they relate to frontal brain maturation; only 37 percent of their lucid dreamers reported being able to manipulate the dream [61][21]. In the Saunders meta-analysis the child subgroup had the highest frequent-dreamer estimate (26 percent, CI 23-29) [21].

#### Sex differences

Sex differences are small and inconsistent: the German representative sample found a female advantage [60], while the meta-analysis found reporting of sex and other sample characteristics too patchy to analyse robustly [21]. Dream recall frequency, which is itself higher in women, explains part of any difference.

# Part B: The science and the evidence


Knowledge-base section for a self-hosted lucid-dreaming journal. Everything below was checked against primary sources (journal abstracts or full texts, PubMed/Europe PMC records, publisher pages) on 2026-11-06. Where a claim could only be confirmed from secondary sources, or could not be confirmed at all, the text says so. Nothing here is medical advice; the app should present it as educational material.

Terminology: a lucid dream (LD) is a dream in which the dreamer knows, during the dream, that they are dreaming. Dream control is a separate, optional feature. A "signal-verified lucid dream" (SVLD) is one in which the sleeper, while in polysomnographically confirmed REM sleep, makes a pre-agreed voluntary eye-movement signal.

---

### 1. Sleep physiology the practice depends on

#### 1.1 Stages and cycles

Human sleep alternates between non-REM (N1, N2, N3) and REM sleep in cycles that average about 90 minutes (first cycle commonly 70-100 min, later ones 90-120 min), with four to six cycles per night [1][2][3]. In a typical young adult, N1 takes 2-5% of sleep time, N2 45-55%, N3 (slow-wave sleep) 13-23% and REM 20-25% [1]. Deep N3 sleep is concentrated in the first cycles; REM is sparse early and dominates later. The first REM period starts roughly 90 minutes after sleep onset and may last only a few minutes, whereas REM periods in the second half of the night lengthen to the order of 30-60 minutes [2][3]. This asymmetry is the single most important physiological fact for induction: almost every method that works is timed to the long, late-night REM periods.

#### 1.2 REM features

REM sleep combines a low-voltage, mixed-frequency, wake-like EEG with rapid eye movements and near-complete postural muscle atonia [1]. The atonia is actively generated: in rodent work, motoneurons are silenced by a combined glycine and GABA (GABA-A and GABA-B) inhibitory drive, and paralysis is reversed only when all three receptor systems are blocked [4]. Eye and respiratory muscles are spared, which is why eye movements can be used as a channel out of the dream (section 2.1). Reviews note that core REM features such as atonia remain intact during lucid REM [78].

#### 1.3 Hypnagogic and hypnopompic states

Sensory experiences at sleep onset (hypnagogic) and on waking (hypnopompic) are common. In a representative UK telephone survey of 4,972 people, 37% reported hypnagogic and 12.5% hypnopompic hallucinations; both were more frequent in people with insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness or mental disorders, and far more common than narcolepsy (0.04%) [5]. Wake-initiated lucid dream (WILD) techniques deliberately try to hold awareness through the hypnagogic transition.

#### 1.4 Sleep paralysis

Sleep paralysis is REM atonia persisting into, or beginning before, conscious wakefulness, often with hallucinations. A systematic review pooling 35 studies and 36,533 participants estimated lifetime prevalence at 7.6% in the general population, 28.3% in students and 31.9% in psychiatric patients [6]. Sleep paralysis and lucid dreaming are positively related: in an online survey of 1,928 adults the association was strongest for sleep-paralysis episodes with vestibular-motor hallucinations, dissociative experiences predicted both, and sleep paralysis (but not lucid dreaming) was additionally tied to poorer sleep quality, anxiety and life stress [7]. Practically, fragmented and interrupted sleep (which WBTB-style induction creates) is a known trigger for sleep paralysis, and practitioners should be told it is unpleasant but physiologically harmless and self-limiting.

#### 1.5 False awakenings

A false awakening is a dream of having woken up. Objective data are sparse. The only dedicated dataset is a web survey of 90 habitual lucid dreamers: 41% reported false awakenings at least monthly, 79% in the past month and 46% in the past week [8]. These rates come from a self-selected lucid-dreamer sample and should not be generalised. The practical lesson for the app: a reality test on every apparent awakening catches false awakenings, which often convert directly into lucid dreams.

#### 1.6 REM rebound

When REM is suppressed, it recovers with interest. In a laboratory study, eight healthy men selectively deprived of REM for three nights (REM reduced to 9.2% of baseline) showed REM at 140.1% of baseline on the first recovery night [9]. Pharmacological suppression shows the same pattern: high-dose THC reduced REM eye-movement density and duration and withdrawal pushed both above baseline [10]; transdermal nicotine in non-smokers cut REM time dose-dependently with a REM increase on the recovery night [11]. REM rebound is why people stopping cannabis, alcohol or REM-suppressing antidepressants report intense dreaming, and why it is sometimes mistaken for a "technique".

#### 1.7 Why wake-back-to-bed works

Wake-back-to-bed (WBTB) means sleeping about 4.5-6 hours, getting up for a period, then returning to sleep. Mechanistically it (a) places the return to sleep inside the long late-night REM periods, (b) raises cortical arousal and prefrontal engagement immediately before REM, and (c) gives a window to rehearse an intention (MILD) while recent dreams are fresh. Laboratory work by Erlacher and Stumbrys (sections 3.4-3.5) is the best direct evidence for the timing claims.

### 2. Neuroscience of lucidity

The best single map of this field is the 2019 review by Baird, Mota-Rolim and Dresler in Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews [15], updated clinically by Tzioridou and colleagues in 2025 [78]. The honest summary from both: EEG studies are small and inconsistent, neuroimaging is scant but points to prefrontal and parietal regions, and whether brain stimulation can induce lucidity remains unresolved.

#### 2.1 Eye-signal verification (LRLR)

The scientific study of lucid dreaming began when Hearne (1978, PhD thesis) and LaBerge and colleagues (1981) had trained subjects make pre-agreed eye movements while in unequivocal REM sleep [15][16]. In LaBerge's study five subjects signalled lucidity in REM; the signal had "observable physical concomitants" on the polysomnogram, allowing dream events to be time-stamped [16]. The standard signal is a full-scale left-right-left-right sweep without pausing (LRLR), which produces a distinctive EOG trace [15]. Everything that follows depends on this method.

#### 2.2 Voss 2009: frontal 40 Hz gamma and the "hybrid state"

Voss, Holzmann, Tuin and Hobson recorded six trained students (Sleep, 2009); lucid REM showed REM-like delta and theta power but higher gamma power than non-lucid REM, peaking around 40 Hz and strongest frontally and frontolaterally, which they interpreted as a "hybrid state" with features of waking and REM [17]. The result rests on three participants with usable lucid data [15]. Baird and colleagues note two caveats: a failed replication in narcolepsy patients [29], and the risk that frontal scalp gamma is contaminated by the saccadic spike potential from eye-muscle contractions, which neither EOG regression nor current-source-density derivation fully removes at anterior sites [15]. In the narcolepsy study, lucid REM actually showed lower delta, theta and alpha power and lower frontal coherence than non-lucid REM [29]. A 2025 multi-laboratory reanalysis with aggressive artifact removal found minimal sensor-level differences between lucid and non-lucid REM; at the source level there were beta (12-30 Hz) decreases in right central and parietal areas including the temporoparietal junction, increased alpha connectivity, and gamma1 (30-36 Hz) increases in right temporo-occipital regions including the precuneus around the moment of eye signalling [28]. The take-home: "40 Hz gamma = lucidity" is an attractive slogan but not an established fact.

#### 2.3 Dresler 2012: fMRI of a lucid dream

Dresler and colleagues recorded four experienced lucid dreamers with simultaneous EEG/fMRI; one participant produced two signal-verified lucid REM episodes long enough to analyse. Compared with his own non-lucid REM, lucid REM showed strong activation in bilateral precuneus, cuneus, parietal lobules, and prefrontal and occipito-temporal cortices, regions normally deactivated in REM [18]. Earlier, the same group had shown that dreamed hand clenching in lucid REM (bracketed by LRLR signals) activated contralateral sensorimotor cortex [19]. This is still, essentially, an N = 1 result and should be described as such.

#### 2.4 Frontopolar cortex and individual differences

Filevich and colleagues (2015) found greater grey-matter volume in frontopolar cortex (BA9/10), plus anterior cingulate, supplementary motor area and hippocampus, in people scoring higher on a lucidity scale, and higher BA9/10 BOLD during a metacognitive thought-monitoring task [20]. Baird, Castelnovo, Gosseries and Tononi (2018, Scientific Reports, not Neuropsychologia) compared frequent lucid dreamers (three to four lucid dreams a week or more) with matched controls: the frequent group showed increased resting-state functional connectivity between left anterior prefrontal cortex and bilateral angular gyrus, middle temporal gyrus and right inferior frontal gyrus, and higher node degree of left aPFC, but no grey-matter difference [21][15]. So the structural finding did not replicate while the connectivity finding is new; both are consistent with a metacognition account of lucidity.

#### 2.5 Siclari 2017 and where dreaming lives

Siclari and colleagues used high-density EEG with serial awakenings and found that dreaming (lucid or not) is associated with reduced low-frequency activity in a posterior cortical "hot zone", with high-frequency activity there tracking specific content; real-time monitoring of this zone predicted dream reports in NREM [26]. Lucidity is then best understood as added prefrontal/parietal metacognitive processing on top of a posterior dream-generation substrate [15].

#### 2.6 The LuCiD scale (Voss 2013)

The Lucidity and Consciousness in Dreams scale has 28 items on a 6-point scale (0-5) loading on eight factors: insight, control, thought, realism, memory, dissociation, negative emotion and positive emotion. Insight is the factor that most sharply separates lucid from non-lucid dreams, followed by thought, control, positive emotion and dissociation [22]. It has been validated in other languages (e.g. Spanish). It is the right instrument for grading how lucid a given dream was, as opposed to how often one is lucid.

#### 2.7 Voss 2014 tACS and the failed replication

Voss and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience, 2014) applied 30 s of frontal transcranial alternating current at 2, 6, 12, 25, 40, 70 or 100 Hz, or sham, after two minutes of stable REM in 27 participants over up to four nights, then woke them for LuCiD ratings. They reported lucid dreams on 77% of 40 Hz trials and 58% of 25 Hz trials, other frequencies ineffective [23][15]. Baird et al. point out that "lucid" was defined by LuCiD factor thresholds, not eye signalling or judged reports, and that participants had no induction training [15]. Blanchette-Carrière and colleagues (2020) attempted a replication with the same 40 Hz paradigm: stimulation was associated with signal-verified and self-rated lucid dreams, but so was the sham procedure; no stimulation effect was detected [24]. An earlier tDCS study (1 mA over DLPFC in 19 participants) found only weak self-rated increases confined to frequent lucid dreamers [25]. Conclusion: no electrical stimulation protocol is currently established as a lucid-dream inducer, and consumer "40 Hz" devices rest on the unreplicated result.

#### 2.8 Two-way communication (Konkoly 2021)

Four independent laboratories showed that sleepers in signal-verified lucid REM can perceive questions (spoken, Morse-coded lights, tactile) and answer with eye movements or facial-muscle contractions. Across 36 individuals and 57 sessions, participants signalled lucidity in 26% of sessions; in 47% of signal-verified lucid episodes at least one question was answered correctly, giving 29 correct answers from six people. In non-lucid REM control trials there were 366 no-response trials, 11 ambiguous, 1 correct and 1 incorrect [27]. This is the strongest evidence that lucid dreamers can process external information and reason in real time.

#### 2.9 Narcolepsy as a window

People with narcolepsy report far more lucid dreams: 77.4% versus 49.1% of controls had ever had one, and 7.6 versus 0.3 per month [29]; a separate telephone study of 60 patients and 919 controls confirmed large effect sizes, and about 70% of patients with lucid dreams said lucidity relieved their nightmares [30].

### 3. Induction techniques: what has evidence

#### 3.1 How common is lucidity without any technique?

A quality-effects meta-analysis of 34 studies (about 24,000 people, 1966-2016) estimated that 55% of people have had at least one lucid dream and 23% have them monthly or more [32]. A representative German sample (N = 919) gave 51% lifetime prevalence, with lucid-dream frequency correlating r = .57 with dream-recall frequency [33]. In a Brazilian online survey (N = 3,427), 77% had experienced lucidity but only 29% reported dream control [31]. Baseline rates matter for the app: a user who already has monthly lucid dreams will respond to techniques differently from a never-lucid user.

#### 3.2 The two systematic reviews

Stumbrys, Erlacher, Schädlich and Schredl (2012) reviewed 35 studies (11 laboratory, 24 field; 26 testing cognitive techniques, 11 external stimulation, 1 a drug). Methodological quality was "relatively low" and "none of the induction techniques were verified to induce lucid dreams reliably and consistently", though some were promising, above all MILD combined with WBTB [34][39]. Later summaries state that of 17 techniques graded, six had support in at least two studies with no contrary evidence, nine were ambiguous or unreplicated and two were ineffective [35]; I could not obtain the review's table to confirm which techniques fell in each bin, so that breakdown is reported second-hand.

Tan and Fan (2023) reviewed 19 post-2010 studies covering 14 techniques, grouped into cognitive techniques, external stimulation, substance intervention and cortical stimulation. They rated MILD the most effective technique, called SSILD and galantamine promising candidates needing replication, treated WBTB as an add-on whose independent effect cannot be separated, and noted that reality testing on its own was ineffective in most studies that included it [35].

#### 3.3 MILD (Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams)

Developed by LaBerge for his 1980 Stanford dissertation: on waking from a dream, recall it, set the intention "next time I'm dreaming I will remember I'm dreaming", and visualise re-entering the dream and becoming lucid. LaBerge reported going from under one lucid dream a month to 18-26 a month with MILD (self-study) [39]. All ten early MILD studies came from LaBerge's group [39].

The National Australian Lucid Dream Induction Study (Aspy et al., 2017; N = 169 completing) tested reality testing (RT) alone, RT + WBTB, and RT + WBTB + MILD over one baseline week and one technique week. The full combination raised lucid dreaming to 17.4% of nights in week 2 from 9.4% in week 1; RT alone was least effective; and when participants fell asleep within 5 minutes of finishing MILD, 45.8% of those attempts produced a lucid dream [37][38]. The 17.4% figure is confirmed from the follow-up paper's introduction; the combined group numbered 47.

The International Lucid Dream Induction Study (Aspy, 2020; 355 completers from 1,618 pre-tested, mostly US, Australia, UK, Canada, Germany, Mexico) compared MILD + WBTB with and without two forms of RT, SSILD + WBTB, and a MILD/SSILD hybrid + WBTB. Mean percentage of nights with a lucid dream rose from 5.3% (baseline week) to 15.8% (technique week) overall. The MILD groups without RT reached 18.4% and 20.2% (weighted MILD average 16.5%); SSILD 16.9%; hybrid 13.3%; the MILD + RT groups were lower (10.8% and 13.4%). Falling asleep within 10 minutes of the technique gave 18.3% versus 11.1% (p = .037, V = 0.10); the 5-minute cut-off was not significant here. Better general dream recall predicted success. Sleep quality on lucid nights was slightly higher (3.6 vs 3.4, p = .037) and did not differ between baseline and technique weeks [38].

Practical translation: an evening-prepared, WBTB-timed MILD with a fast return to sleep is the best-supported home protocol, with roughly one lucid dream per 5-6 attempts in motivated samples.

#### 3.4 Wake-back-to-bed: duration and timing

Erlacher and Stumbrys (2020) woke 51 unselected sport students from REM after about 6 hours of sleep and compared a 60-minute wake period doing dream work + MILD (two cohorts: 54.5% and 53.3% self-rated lucid dreams; 27% signal-verified), 30 minutes of the same (35.7%; 14% verified), and 60 minutes of reading (9%) or Wii balance games (0%) in the subsequent nap. They concluded that about one hour of WBTB spent on dream work and MILD is most efficient, and that the content of the waking period matters, not just being awake [39]. The older LaBerge, Phillips and Levitan (1994) nap studies underlie the widely quoted 30-120 minute window, with 10 minutes or 4 hours awake being less effective [39]. A follow-up (Erlacher et al., 2022; 50 participants) moved the interruption earlier, to 4.5 hours: MILD after a REM awakening gave 26% (13% verified), MILD after a time-based awakening 0%, and reality testing after a REM awakening 20%; overall 7 of 50 (14%), with 5 LRLR-verified. The authors concluded that "the earlier sleep interruption seems to reduce the lucid dream induction rate", while noting other protocol changes confounded the comparison [40]. For the app, recommend WBTB after roughly 5-6 hours of sleep, ideally waking from a dream, 30-60 minutes awake, used mainly on free days.

#### 3.5 SSILD (Senses-Initiated Lucid Dream)

A community technique published in 2011 by the pseudonymous "Cosmic Iron" on a Chinese forum and in English in 2012: after WBTB, lie in bed and cycle attention through sight (closed eyes), hearing and bodily sensation in several short and then longer cycles, then let yourself sleep. Its only controlled test is ILDIS, where it matched MILD (16.9% of nights) and the hybrid did no better than either [38]. Tan and Fan rate it "insufficient/ambiguous" because it rests on one study [35]. Reasonable to offer, with that caveat.

#### 3.6 Reality testing and critical reflection (Tholey)

Tholey (1983) proposed the "reflection technique": ask "Am I dreaming or not?" critically and repeatedly through the day, with a critical-reflective attitude that transfers into the dream; he reported that previously non-lucid subjects gained lucidity after a median of 4-5 weeks of combined practice (reflection, intention, autosuggestion) [36]. Modern evidence for reality testing as a standalone method is poor: it was least effective in the Australian study [37], added nothing to MILD + WBTB in ILDIS [38], and was ineffective in five of six recent studies that included it, except when paired with sound cues during sleep [35][44]. Reality testing is best framed as a low-cost habit that supports lucidity recognition (especially after false awakenings), not as an inducer by itself.

#### 3.7 WILD and related transitional methods

Wake-initiated lucid dreaming (maintaining awareness through sleep onset, usually after WBTB or in a nap) is well attested in lab reports: LaBerge's early work documented lucid dreams entered from N1 and N2 that were confirmed by LRLR signalling [15]. As a trainable, general-population technique, however, it has not been separately evaluated in a controlled study; the 2024 Russian-language review lists dream-exit/re-entry (DEILD) with SSILD and Tholey's technique as "promising, requiring further research" [35]. Treat WILD as advanced and anecdotal in efficacy, and warn that it is the route most likely to pass through sleep paralysis and hypnagogic imagery.

#### 3.8 Dream journaling and dream recall

Recall is a prerequisite and a predictor. Lucid-dream frequency correlates with dream-recall frequency (r = .57 in a representative sample) [33], and better recall predicted induction success in ILDIS [38]. Aspy (2016) showed that single-question retrospective measures underestimate recall and that keeping a logbook enhances it, with the disparity largest in people whose recall improved during the logbook period [48]. There is no trial showing journaling alone induces lucidity; its evidence is as a foundation that raises recall and the pool of dreams in which lucidity can occur.

#### 3.9 Meditation and mindfulness

Baird, Riedner, Boly, Davidson and Tononi (2019) compared 38 long-term meditators with 140 meditation-naive adults: meditators reported 1.11 versus 0.45 lucid dreams per month (about 2.5 times more; beta = 0.58, p = .03) with no difference in dream recall; in meditators, lucidity tracked the observing and decentering facets of trait mindfulness. But randomising the naive group to an 8-week MBSR course, an active health-education control or waitlist produced no change in lucid-dream frequency at post-test or follow-up [46]. So trait mindfulness correlates with lucidity; a standard 8-week course does not cause it.

#### 3.10 Video games and other correlates

Gackenbach (2006) reported that frequent video-game players have more lucid dreams than non-players, in in-class and online samples; the work is correlational and later reanalyses dropped some comparisons for ceiling effects [47]. Lucid dreaming also correlates with internal locus of control, need for cognition and creativity [82]. None of these are interventions.

#### 3.11 External cues during REM: light, sound, smell, apps

Light: LaBerge and Levitan (1995) validated the DreamLight (later NovaDreamer) mask by alternating cue and no-cue nights without the sleeper's knowledge: 11 subjects reported 32 lucid dreams, 22 on cue nights and 10 on non-cue nights [41]. The NovaDreamer went out of production in 2004 and no current consumer mask has comparable published validation.

Sound, paired with pre-sleep training ("targeted lucidity reactivation", TLR): Carr, Konkoly, Mallett, Edwards, Appel and Blagrove paired a sound and light cue with a lucid mindset for 20 minutes before a morning nap, then replayed the cue in REM; 50% of participants had a signal-verified lucid dream in a single nap, including three with no prior lucid dreams (published in Psychology of Consciousness: Theory, Research, and Practice, 2023, advance online 2020; not Current Biology) [42]. Erlacher et al. (2020) paired ring tones with reality testing in the lab: 5 self-rated lucid dreams versus 1 under sham, 2 signal-verified (16.7%) [44]. An olfactory-cue version yielded 2 of 16 (12.5%) and was judged unpromising [45].

Apps: Konkoly et al. (2024) ported TLR to an Android app that trains the cue before bed and replays it from 6 hours after training. Experiment 1 (n = 19): lucid dreams rose from 0.74 to 2.11 per week (p = .007), with 14 of 19 reporting at least one versus 8 the week before. Experiment 2 (n = 120 with at least one night in each condition, 416 downloads): all groups were cued on night 1; on night 2 the group that kept receiving trained cues held its rate (OR = 1.0) while control groups receiving untrained or no cues declined (combined OR = 3.4, p = .004). Cues produced measurable sleep disruption on accelerometry, and the authors flag expectancy and arousal as alternative explanations [43]. This is the only peer-reviewed evidence for any lucid-dreaming app; commercial app claims beyond it are unverified.

### 4. Substances

The app should not recommend drugs or supplements. The evidence is summarised so that users encounter accurate information rather than forum lore. See also the 2024 narrative review by Oldoni and colleagues [54].

#### 4.1 Galantamine

Galantamine is a prescription acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (licensed for Alzheimer's disease) that increases cholinergic tone, which drives REM sleep. LaBerge, LaMarca and Baird (2018, PLOS ONE) ran a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover in 121 experienced, high-recall participants: on three nights, in counterbalanced order, they woke after about 4.5 hours, took 0, 4 or 8 mg, stayed up about 30 minutes, then practised MILD. Lucid dreams were reported by 14% on placebo, 27% on 4 mg (OR 2.29) and 42% on 8 mg (OR 4.46, 95% CI 2.38-8.35); 57% had a lucid dream on at least one active night. Mild side effects occurred in 12% on active doses versus 3% on placebo: gastrointestinal upset, nausea (n = 5), difficulty returning to sleep (n = 5), insomnia, next-day fatigue. Asthma, beta-blocker use, cardiac arrhythmia and severe mental illness were exclusion criteria, and sleep was not objectively measured [52]. A smaller eight-night home study (N = 35) found 40% lucid with WBTB + galantamine and 34% with meditation/dream-reliving + galantamine versus 9% and 11% with placebo [53][54]. Prescribing information for galantamine warns of vagotonic effects on cardiac conduction (bradycardia, heart block, syncope, dose-related), gastrointestinal effects, and advises monitoring in severe asthma or COPD, seizure disorders and risk of GI bleeding [56]. Because the 14% placebo arm already included WBTB + MILD, the drug effect is on top of the best cognitive protocol, which is why the effect looks large.

#### 4.2 Other cholinergics: choline, alpha-GPC, huperzine A, nicotine

Choline and alpha-GPC are sold as "natural galantamine". The only controlled test of alpha-GPC (33 participants, no mental training) found no effect: 6 lucid dreams split evenly between active and placebo nights [54]. Huperzine A appears only in a patent application [54]. Nicotine patches worn overnight are promoted in some books and forums; there is no study of nicotine and lucid dreaming. What is known: 24-hour patches in smokers increased wake time and micro-arousals, reduced REM percentage and produced more vivid visual dream reports [12]; in 12 non-smokers 7 and 14 mg patches caused early-morning awakening and dose-dependent REM loss, with REM rebound on the recovery night [11]; patch labels advise removal at bedtime if vivid dreams or sleep disturbance occur. Nicotine is addictive and nonsmokers tolerate it poorly; the app should state plainly that this practice is unsafe and unsupported.

#### 4.3 Vitamin B6

Aspy, Madden and Delfabbro (2018) randomised 100 Australians to 240 mg pyridoxine, a B-complex, or placebo for five nights: B6 increased the amount of dream content recalled (the author quotes about 64% more), without changing vividness, bizarreness, colour or sleep variables; the B-complex group reported worse sleep quality and more tiredness. Lucidity was not increased [55]. Long-term high-dose B6 carries a known risk of peripheral neuropathy, which the app should mention if it mentions B6 at all.

#### 4.4 Melatonin

There are no studies of melatonin as a lucid-dream inducer [54]. In two small randomised crossover trials in patients with pathologically reduced REM, 3 mg melatonin for four weeks raised REM percentage from about 14% to about 18% versus a fall on placebo [13]; vivid-dream reports with melatonin are anecdotal and consistent with REM effects. Its effect on healthy sleepers' REM is small and inconsistent.

#### 4.5 Cannabis

THC suppresses REM eye-movement density and, to a lesser degree, REM duration; withdrawal produces a rebound above baseline [10]. Heavy users abstaining show lower total sleep and slow-wave sleep and shortened REM latency on night 2 [14]. The vivid dreams after stopping cannabis are REM rebound, not a technique, and they fade within weeks.

#### 4.6 Other substances

Mugwort, Calea ternifolia, Silene capensis, 5-HTP and similar "dream herbs" have ethnographic reports or single old experiments on recall and vividness, and no lucid-dreaming trials [54]. Pregabalin, L-dopa and dopamine agonists have case reports of vivid or lucid dreams and nightmares as adverse effects [54].

### 5. Benefits and applications

#### 5.1 Nightmares

This is the best-developed clinical application, and the evidence is encouraging but thin. Zadra and Pihl (1997) treated five recurrent-nightmare sufferers with lucid-dream induction (two also had relaxation and imagery); at one year, four were nightmare-free and one improved, and the authors noted it was unclear whether lucidity itself or the capacity to change the dream did the work [58]. Spoormaker and van den Bout (2006) randomised 23 chronic nightmare sufferers to a single 2-hour individual session, a 2-hour group session, or waitlist; at 12 weeks nightmare frequency fell in both treatment groups, sleep quality and PTSD severity did not change, and several responders never became lucid [57]. Holzinger, Klösch and Saletu (2015) randomised 32 patients to Gestalt therapy with or without lucid-dreaming training over ten weeks; both groups improved, with the combined group framed as more potent [59]. A later open study in PTSD found anxiety and depression decreased during lucid-dreaming therapy, but sleep variables did not change and nightmare-severity change did not track trauma-symptom change [60]. In a large self-help trial, adding a lucid-dreaming module (and sleep hygiene) to imagery rehearsal therapy did not improve on IRT alone and the IRT-only arm did best, though dropout was very high [61]. Reviews conclude that most studies report reduced nightmare frequency (Ouchene 2023: 4 RCTs, 2 case series, 5 case reports, 10 of 11 positive) but that internal validity is limited, samples are tiny, and lucidity may not be the active ingredient [62][63]. In narcolepsy, 70% of patients with lucid dreams said lucidity relieved their nightmares [30]. A fair app statement: lucid-dreaming therapy is a promising adjunct for recurrent nightmares; imagery rehearsal therapy remains the better-evidenced first-line treatment.

#### 5.2 Motor-skill practice

Three small studies show practice in a lucid dream can improve waking performance. Erlacher and Schredl (2010): 40 participants assigned to lucid practice, physical practice or control on a coin-tossing task; the seven who managed to practise in a lucid dream improved [64]. Stumbrys, Erlacher and Schredl (2016): 68 participants, finger-tapping; lucid, mental and physical practice groups all improved overnight, control did not, and sleep quality was unaffected [65]. Schädlich, Erlacher and Schredl (2017), darts in the sleep lab: only lucid practisers with few in-dream distractions improved (18%, d = 3.84, n = 4) [66]. Effects are plausible and consistent with mental-practice literature, but samples are single digits.

#### 5.3 Creativity, problem solving, mood

In an online survey of 301 lucid dreamers, the most common application was simply having fun, followed by nightmare resolution, problem solving, creativity and practising skills [67]. Lucidity correlates with creativity and need for cognition in cross-sectional work [82], but no experiment has shown that cultivating lucidity increases waking creativity. On mood: a five-week diary study of 149 participants found people felt more refreshed, not less, on mornings after lucid dreams [68], and ILDIS found slightly higher sleep-quality ratings on lucid nights [38]. Lucid-dream intensity (control, certainty, positive emotion) is inversely associated with depression, anxiety and stress, whereas mere frequency is not [71].

#### 5.4 Does lucid dreaming harm sleep?

Frequent lucid dreaming has been linked to poorer self-rated sleep quality in two cross-sectional samples (N = 444 and N = 1,380) [80], and to night awakenings and snooze-button use [81]. A four-study multicentre project found that self-assessed awakenings, polyphasic sleep and physiologically validated wake-to-REM transitions were associated with lucid dreaming, whereas sleep quality and validated awakening counts were not [77]. Against this, the induction studies that measured it found no deterioration in sleep quality when lucidity was achieved [38][65][68], and a 489-person survey found no association between lucid-dream frequency and worse sleep or dissociation [75]. The most defensible reading: lucid dreams themselves do not degrade sleep; sleep-interrupting induction methods can, and people with fragmented sleep are more likely to become lucid, which inflates cross-sectional correlations [69][70].

### 6. Risks, contraindications and safe-use guidance

#### 6.1 What the cautionary literature says

Vallat and Ruby (2019) argued that cultivating lucidity is not obviously benign because the main methods fragment sleep and because lucid REM is a hybrid state whose long-term effects are unknown [69]. Soffer-Dudek (2020) called the field's focus on benefits a shared blind spot and asked for experimental work on harms, particularly in people high in baseline dissociation or schizotypy [70].

The key empirical study is Aviram and Soffer-Dudek (2018; 187 students, 78 followed for two months). Spontaneous lucid-dream frequency was unrelated to psychopathology. Deliberate induction attempts correlated with stress (r = .21), sleep problems (r = .23), obsessive-compulsive symptoms (r = .15) and dissociation (r = .16); among the 64 people using induction techniques, technique use correlated with sleep problems (r = .37), schizotypy (r = .34), depression (r = .35), dissociation (r = .32) and OC symptoms (r = .26). Longitudinally, the frequency of induced (not spontaneous) lucid dreams predicted increases in dissociation (beta = .26) and schizotypy (beta = .14) two months later, controlling for baseline [71]. Effects are small, self-reported and from one student sample, but the direction is consistent.

Depression: Taitz (2011) found lucid-dream frequency positively correlated with depression in undergraduates (p < .001), the opposite of the therapeutic hypothesis [73]; a 163-person survey plus interviews argued for therapeutic potential [74]. Directionality is unknown: depressed people have more disturbed sleep and more awakenings, which themselves raise lucidity.

Psychosis: Mota and colleagues (2016) compared 25 people with schizophrenia, 20 with bipolar I and 28 controls. Lifetime lucid-dream prevalence was similar (48%, 55%, 46%), but among lucid dreamers, dream control was reported by 67% and 73% of the clinical groups versus 23% of controls. The authors warn that lucid-dream practice could "further empower deliria and hallucinations" in psychotic populations [72]. Holzinger's clinical group likewise advises caution with psychotic clients [70].

Sleep paralysis and dysphoric lucid dreams: a content analysis of 400 forum posts found benefits tied to successful induction and high dream control, and the main complaints were lucid dysphoria (25 posts), poor sleep (24) and sleep paralysis, mostly after failed induction or low-control lucid dreams [76]. In a survey of 489 mostly lucid dreamers, about 10% of lucid dreams were emotionally negative and frequency was not linked to worse sleep or dissociation [75].

#### 6.2 Who should not practise, or should get advice first

Based on the above, the app should advise against induction practice, or recommend discussing it with a clinician, for people who:

- have a psychotic disorder or bipolar disorder, or a history of psychotic episodes [72]
- have a dissociative disorder or marked derealisation/depersonalisation [71][70]
- have an active sleep disorder (insomnia, untreated sleep apnoea, narcolepsy under treatment) or are already sleep-deprived [69]
- are in acute depression, grief or PTSD without clinical support (nightmare work is best done with a therapist) [60][73]
- are under 18, pregnant, or have cardiac, respiratory or seizure conditions, if any substance is contemplated [52][56]

#### 6.3 Safe-use guidance the app can display

1. Sleep comes first. Never shorten total sleep to make room for WBTB; schedule it on nights when you can sleep in afterwards.
2. Use WBTB at most two or three nights a week, and stop if daytime sleepiness, mood or concentration suffer for more than a few days.
3. Keep the WBTB wake period to 30-60 minutes of quiet dream work, not screens or stimulation [39].
4. If an induction attempt fails, let it go; most negative experiences come from forcing it [76].
5. Sleep paralysis is harmless and ends on its own; relax, breathe, wiggle a toe or finger, or use it as a gateway if you are comfortable doing so [6][7].
6. Do a reality check on every awakening; it catches false awakenings [8].
7. Do not use nicotine, prescription cholinesterase inhibitors or high-dose supplements for dreaming. Galantamine is a prescription drug with cardiac and respiratory cautions, and the app does not recommend it [52][56].
8. If lucid dreams become distressing, if waking reality starts to feel dreamlike, or if you notice increasing detachment, pause the practice and talk to a professional [71][70].

### 7. Measuring lucidity and recall (for in-app self-assessment)

#### 7.1 Definitions to show users

The standard definition used in questionnaires: "In a lucid dream, one is aware that one is dreaming during the dream. Thus it is possible to wake up deliberately, or to influence the action of the dream actively, or to observe the course of the dream passively" (Schredl & Erlacher 2004 wording, as reproduced in MADRE) [50]. Snyder and Gackenbach (1988) argued that vague definitions distort prevalence estimates and established the convention that "frequent lucid dreamers" are those with at least one lucid dream a month [51][15].

#### 7.2 Lucid Dream Frequency Scale (Stumbrys, Erlacher & Schredl 2013)

An eight-point item: 0 = never, 1 = less than once a year, 2 = about once a year, 3 = about 2 to 4 times a year, 4 = about once a month, 5 = about 2 to 3 times a month, 6 = about once a week, 7 = several times a week. In 93 sport students retested after four weeks, test-retest reliability was r = .89 for lucid dreams and r = .75 for the parallel nightmare item [49][50]. For analysis it is converted to lucid dreams per month: 0, 0.042, 0.083, 0.25, 1.0, 2.5, 4.0, 8.0 [46]. This is the recommended frequency item for onboarding and periodic re-assessment.

#### 7.3 Mannheim Dream Questionnaire (MADRE; Schredl et al. 2014)

MADRE packages the frequency items above with dream recall, nightmares, attitudes and dream use. Dream recall uses a seven-point scale (0 = never, 1 = less than once a month, 2 = about once a month, 3 = 2 to 3 times a month, 4 = about once a week, 5 = several times a week, 6 = almost every morning); recall is converted to mornings per week (0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.625, 1.0, 3.5, 6.5) [46][50]. In the 2,929-person validation sample, 38.5% had never had a lucid dream, 10.5% had them about monthly and 3.2% almost every morning; retest interval averaged 14 days, internal consistency of the attitude scale was .91, and lucid-dream frequency correlated r = .36 with dream recall [50]. MADRE is free to reuse and is the best source for comparable norms.

#### 7.4 LuCiD scale (Voss et al. 2013)

For rating a single dream: 28 items, 0-5, eight factors (insight, control, thought, realism, memory, dissociation, negative emotion, positive emotion). Insight items ("I was aware that I was dreaming") separate lucid from non-lucid dreams most strongly [22]. An app can use the insight and control subscales as a per-dream lucidity grade, and the full scale for research export.

#### 7.5 Dream Lucidity Questionnaire (DLQ; Stumbrys, Erlacher & Schredl 2013)

Developed for the tDCS study, the DLQ rates aspects of lucidity within a dream (awareness of dreaming, control over content, access to waking memory) on a 0-4 scale; sources describe it as 12 or 13 items and I could not confirm the count from the original [25]. It is shorter than LuCiD and suited to morning check-ins.

#### 7.6 Measuring recall properly

Retrospective single questions underestimate recall relative to logbooks, and logbooks themselves raise recall [48]. For the app this means: track recall from the journal (dreams per morning) rather than from a one-off question, show users that early journal weeks will inflate their apparent "improvement", and keep a separate retrospective item for comparability with published norms.

### 1. Dream signs

#### 1.1 LaBerge's definition

LaBerge and Rheingold introduce "dreamsigns" as the peculiar, recurring features of your own dreams that can serve as cues to recognise the dreaming state. The book's own illustration is a "mutant lens" that kept multiplying in the author's dreams: "I resolved that in future dreams like this I would notice the mutant lens as a dreamsign. And indeed, I have become lucid in at least a dozen dreams by recognizing this particular oddity. Each of us has his or her own individual dreamsigns, though some are familiar to most of us, like the case of going to work in your pajamas" [1]. The course workbook gives the compact definition: "Dreamsigns are objects or events that are impossible or improbable in waking reality" [2].

The Lucidity Institute reports an empirical motivation for the whole exercise: "In a NightLight experiment studying the role of dreamsigns in the initiation of lucidity, we found that people were more likely to reach lucidity in a dream if they showed a high awareness of the strangeness of dreamsigns" [2]. (The NightLight was the Institute's newsletter; the underlying study is an in-house survey, not a peer-reviewed trial.)

#### 1.2 The Dreamsign Inventory: four categories and sub-types

LaBerge states that the inventory "lists types of dreamsigns organized according to the way people naturally seem to categorize their experiences in dreams. There are four primary categories" [1]. The first (inner awareness) covers what the dreamer perceives as happening inside themselves; the other three (action, form, context) classify elements of the dream environment. The sub-types and example quotations are taken directly from the book [1]:

| Category | Book definition (paraphrased) | Sub-types in the inventory | Example dream reports quoted in the book |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inner awareness | "You have a peculiar thought, a strong emotion, feel an unusual sensation, or have altered perceptions." Thoughts that could only occur in a dream or that "magically" affect the dream world; inappropriate or overwhelming emotion; paralysis, out-of-body feelings, sudden sexual arousal; perception unusually clear or fuzzy. | Thoughts, Emotions, Sensations, Perceptions | "When I found the door locked, I 'wished' it open." / "I am filled with extreme anxiety and remorse." / "I seem to lift 'out of body'." / "Somehow I could see perfectly without my glasses." |

LaBerge emphasises that context dreamsigns often consist of ordinary elements in odd combination: "Sometimes in dreams the combination of elements - people, places, actions, or things, is odd, although there is nothing inherently strange about any item by itself" [1]. Community guides often abbreviate the categories as IA / A / F / C in the margin of a journal [46].

For an app, this gives a ready two-level taxonomy: category (4) x sub-type (18). It is worth storing both, because the book's "target" is chosen at category level while sub-types make the tag list more specific.

#### 1.3 The cataloging exercise ("dreamsign inventory" exercise)

The book's EXERCISE: CATALOGING YOUR DREAMSIGNS has five steps [1]:

1. Keep a dream journal: "Keep a journal in which you record all of your dreams. When you have collected at least a dozen dreams, proceed to the next step."
2. Catalog your dreamsigns: "While continuing to collect dreams, mark the dreamsigns in your dream reports. Underline them, and list them after each dream description."
3. Classify each dreamsign using the dreamsign inventory: write its category next to it ("if you dreamed of a person with the head of a cat, this would be a form dreamsign").
4. Pick target dreamsign categories: "Count how many times each dreamsign category (inner awareness, action, form, or context) occurs and rank them by frequency. Whichever occurs most often will be your target dreamsign category in the next step. If there is a tie between categories, pick the one that appeals to you."
5. Practice looking for dreamsigns while awake: "Make a habit of examining your daily life for events that fit under your dreamsign category."

The 1995 course version (Unit 1, Exercise 2 "Dreamsign Awareness") refines this [2]: read your last 10 dreams and underline or circle each dreamsign; list them on a "Dreamsign List" chart with date, text and a tick-box for Awareness / Action / Form / Context; then select two sub-categories as targets ("Research has shown that these subcategories are frequently associated with lucidity"). The course's Target Dreamsigns form offers six tick-boxes: Awareness, Action, Form, Context, plus two device-specific targets, "Lights" and "Mechanical Malfunctions". For one week or 10 dreams (whichever is longer), the user lists each target dreamsign found, with date, and rates it on the Dreamsign Awareness Scale:

| DSA rating | Course wording [2] |
|---|---|
| 0 | No awareness: you only noticed the dreamsign was odd after you were awake. |
| 1 | Odd: you noticed it was odd during the dream but did not try to explain it. |
| 2 | Semilucid: you noticed it was odd and tried to explain it, but did not become lucid. |
| 3 | Lucid: you noticed it was odd and realised therefore that you were dreaming. |
| 4 | Post-lucid: you were already aware you were dreaming when the dreamsign occurred. |

Step 7 of the exercise adds a "Waking Life Dreamsigns" list: carry a notebook and record waking anomalies that resemble your targets, to train noticing [2]. This 0-4 scale is a directly implementable per-tag field ("how aware was I of this sign in this dream?") and is the best-documented lucidity-related rating in the LaBerge material.

The course also includes a worked sample: an eight-line swim-practice dream is marked with eight dreamsigns labelled CONTEXT (at swim practice as if 12 years ago; no swimsuit), ACTION (find one and put it on; a boy hurts me), FORM (pool becomes a sea; waterfall edge like a dam; the boy gets older) and INNER AWARENESS (I have a crush on the boy) [2]. This is a useful fixture for testing an auto-tagger.

#### 1.4 Dream signs in MILD and reality testing

MILD (Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams) uses dreamsigns in step 4. The book's MILD TECHNIQUE [1]: (1) set up dream recall before bed; (2) on waking from a dream, recall as many details as possible; (3) while returning to sleep, "concentrate singlemindedly on your intention to remember to recognize that you're dreaming. Tell yourself: 'Next time I'm dreaming, I want to remember I'm dreaming'"; (4) "imagine that you are back in the dream from which you have just awakened, but this time you recognize that it is a dream. Find a dreamsign in the experience; when you see it say to yourself: 'I'm dreaming!' and continue your fantasy"; (5) repeat 3 and 4 until the intention is set. The commentary recommends that deep sleepers get up for 10 to 15 minutes and "write out your dream and read it over, noting all dreamsigns, in preparation for the MILD visualization" [1]. An app can support this by showing the just-written dream's tagged signs on a "MILD rehearsal" screen.

In the Reflection-Intention technique (LaBerge's adaptation of Tholey), step 3 says: "Observe your environment carefully for your target dreamsigns from chapter 2. Imagine what it would be like if a dreamsign from your target category were present" [1]. The Critical State Testing technique tells the reader to "Pick five to ten different occasions during the day to test your state. These should be circumstances that are similar in some ways to your dreams. Any time you come in contact with something that resembles a dreamsign, test your state ... If you have recurrent dreams, any situations related to the recurrent content are ideal" [1]. The worked example ("Joe Dreamer") tests his state when he steps into an elevator, speaks to his boss, sees an attractive woman, reads a typographical error, and goes to the bathroom ("bathrooms are often quite strange in his dreams") [1]. This is the rationale for a feature that lets the user attach a custom reminder to a habitual moment linked to a dream sign.

#### 1.5 Tholey's critical question

Paul Tholey developed the Reflection Technique in 1959 and published it in English in 1983 [3]. In his own words: "the subject asks him or herself several times during the course of the day: 'Am I awake, or am I dreaming?'. The purpose is to achieve a generally critical attitude towards one's state of consciousness thus when confronted with unusual experiences this facilitates recognition of the dreaming state" [4]. He had his first lucid dream after four weeks, triggered by seeing a long-dead aunt [4]. He notes that with practice "active questioning ... ultimately gives rise to a passively receptive focus on current experience which, in turn, makes the posing of critical questions a superfluous matter" [4].

Tholey stresses pairing the question with an actual test. He lists physical tests (turn 180 degrees and try to stand still; in a dream "the body continues to turn ... or the surroundings begin to revolve") and mental tests: "One of the most effective tests is trying to remember what has happened during the immediately preceding period of time. Should one experience bizarre events or a lapse of memory, this may indicate that one is dreaming." Because a memory test fails against false awakenings, he recommends "turning a light on ... upon waking up. If the light does not go on, this may signify a dream state" [4]. His "combined technique" added intention and autosuggestion [3][4]. LaBerge quotes Tholey's claim that subjects who have never had a lucid dream "will have the first one after a median time of 4 to 5 [weeks], with great interindividual deviation" and that most who persist "experience at least one lucid dream every night" [1]; this is Tholey's self-report, not a controlled result. LaBerge's own guidance adds: "Ask yourself the critical question as often as possible (at least the five to ten specific times you selected ...): 'Am I dreaming or awake?' Don't just automatically ask the question and mindlessly reply, 'Obviously, I'm awake,' or you will do the same thing when you actually are dreaming" [1].

#### 1.6 Historical observers: Hervey de Saint-Denys and van Eeden

Hervey de Saint-Denys published *Les rêves et les moyens de les diriger* anonymously in Paris in 1867; the 1982 English edition (*Dreams and How to Guide Them*, trans. N. Fry, ed. M. Schatzman, Duckworth) is an abridgement of the 1964 Tchou edition [5]. He began recording dreams daily in his early teens [5]. Malamud's 1983 review describes the book as "more than twenty years of experimentation with his own lucid dreams", notes his "ability to recall an unbroken sequence of images no matter when he was awakened", and summarises his central observation: he traced "every image and transition to associatively linked waking memories", concluding that dreams are "essentially continuous with waking thought, although different in form" [5a]. He also ran association experiments, using "seven or eight different fragrances as stimuli to evoke associated ideas in his dreams" [5a]. Claims circulating online that he filled "22 volumes" or logged "1,946 nights" could not be verified from any source consulted and are not asserted here.

Frederik van Eeden coined the term "lucid dream" in his 1913 paper "A Study of Dreams", presented to the Society for Psychical Research. He studied his own dreams between 20 January 1898 and 26 December 1912, recording the ones he considered most important; 352 of them are classed as lucid [6][6a]. Both men are the origin of the practice that a dreamer who keeps a long diary notices recurring features in their own dreams; the specific recurring-feature typology, however, is LaBerge's (section 1.2).

### 2. Dream content coding and typical dream themes

#### 2.1 The Hall and Van de Castle (HVDC) system

Calvin Hall collected thousands of dream reports at Case Western Reserve University from the 1940s and, with Robert Van de Castle, formalised the coding categories in *The Content Analysis of Dreams* (1966) [7][8]. The system treats a dream report as a story with a cast, interactions and outcomes [8]. The full rulebook is reproduced online with Domhoff's permission [9a]; the categories and codes (verified against that text):

Identity: immediate family (F father, M mother, X parents, B brother, T sister, H husband, W wife, A son, D daughter, C child, I infant, Y family member), R relative, K known, P prominent, O occupational, E ethnic/national, S stranger, U uncertain. | Social interactions | Aggression A1-A8 (A1 covert hostility ... A6 chasing/capturing/confining, A7 attempted physical harm, A8 aggression resulting in death). | Settings | Location I indoor, O outdoor, A ambiguous, NS none; familiarity F familiar, D distorted, G geographical, U unfamiliar, Q questionable (e.g. "IF", "OU"). |

Domhoff notes "very high intercoder reliability" because the categories are nominal and clearly defined [8]. Cross-cultural studies with the system (US, Canada, Europe, India, Japan, Latin America) consistently find more aggression than friendliness, more misfortune than good fortune and more negative than positive emotion [8]. Men's dreams show more physical aggression [8a].

#### 2.3 DreamBank and the continuity hypothesis

DreamBank.net, run by Adam Schneider and G. William Domhoff at UC Santa Cruz, holds over 20,000 dream reports from dreamers aged 7 to 74, anonymised, with a word-string search engine, a random-sample tool and (for approved researchers) an HVDC coding search. All data are licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0: attribution required, commercial use prohibited [10][11]. For a self-hosted non-commercial journal, DreamBank series can be used as a comparison corpus if attributed; shipping them inside a commercial product is not allowed.

Domhoff's continuity hypothesis, which he traces to Hall and a coauthor in the early 1970s, holds that dream content is continuous with the dreamer's waking conceptions and concerns rather than with literal daily events [12]. In the Domhoff and Schneider long-series case studies (one dreamer with 4,329 reports from ages 12 to 25, another with 664), they found "considerable continuity between the frequency with which specific people and avocations appear in a dream series and the intensity of waking personal concerns about those people and avocations" [12a]. The practical consequence for an app: frequency of a character or setting across the journal is a meaningful signal, and it is the frequency, not single occurrences, that research has validated. Critics (Schredl, in a 2017 *Dreaming* commentary) object that instruments for measuring the intensity of waking concerns are lacking [12b], so an app should present such links as patterns, not findings.

#### 2.5 Recurrent dreams

Secondary sources consistently put lifetime prevalence of at least one recurrent dream series in adults at roughly 60 to 80% (citing Zadra's 1990s work), and Zadra's 1996 study of 110 adults found 77.3% of recurrent dreams were disturbing, with "escapes and pursuits" the top adult theme at 25.9%, followed by accidents/misfortunes (19.7%) and aggression/violence (19.0%) [17]. Gauchat, Séguin, McSween-Cadieux and Zadra (2015) extended this to young adolescents, noting being-chased themes are common in both child and adult recurrent dreams [17]. The exact "60 to 75%" wording sometimes quoted could not be traced to a primary source. For the app, a "recurrent dream" flag (same narrative repeated) is distinct from a recurring dream sign (same element across different dreams) and both should be supported.

#### 2.6 Nightmares versus bad dreams

Zadra and Donderi (2000) were the first to separate nightmares (disturbing dreams that wake the dreamer) from bad dreams (disturbing dreams without awakening); in 89 participants keeping 4-week logs, log-based annual frequencies were significantly higher than retrospective 1- and 12-month estimates, and nightmare frequency correlated more with poorer well-being than bad-dream frequency [18]. Robert and Zadra (2014) analysed 253 nightmares and 431 bad dreams from 331 adults drawn from 9,796 logged reports: physical aggression was the most frequent nightmare theme while interpersonal conflict predominated in bad dreams; nightmares were rated substantially more intense, more bizarre, with more aggression, failure and unfortunate endings; 35% of nightmares and 55% of bad dreams had a primary emotion other than fear [19]. Design implication: log "woke me up" as a boolean, let the primary emotion be anything (not only fear), and compute nightmare and bad-dream frequencies from the log rather than asking the user to estimate.

#### 2.7 Emotions in dreams

Merritt, Stickgold, Pace-Schott, Williams and Hobson (1994) collected 200 reports from 20 subjects using a form that explicitly asked for emotions; this yielded roughly ten times more emotion than earlier studies, about 3.7 emotions per dream, no male/female difference, and a correlation between bizarreness and major shifts in emotion [20]. Later reviews citing Merritt put the share of dreams containing emotion at 70 to 95% (other studies range up to 98.4%) with fear/anxiety the most common when judges rate reports [20][21]. Sikka and colleagues showed the measurement method drives the result: self-ratings produce more emotional dreams and more positive emotion, while external raters find mostly negative emotion because positive emotion is under-reported in narrative text [21]. In 552 home dreams, the majority were positively valenced by self-rating but negatively valenced by external rating [21a]. Consequence: an app should ask for an explicit emotion rating per dream (and ideally per scene) rather than inferring it only from the text; a lexicon run over the narrative will skew negative.

#### 2.8 Bizarreness

Hobson, Hoffman, Helfand and Kostner (1987) defined bizarreness as improbability or impossibility in plot, cognition and affect, scored as incongruity, discontinuity or uncertainty [22]. Revonsuo and Salmivalli (1995) proposed a two-step method: identify every dream element, mark it bizarre or not, and sort bizarre elements into incongruous (contextual, exotic, impossible), vague, or discontinuous [22a]. The HVDC "distorted setting" and "changed form" codes capture the same thing. A single 0-10 or 1-5 "bizarreness" slider plus the LaBerge categories is sufficient for a journal.

#### 2.9 Day residue and dream lag

The day-residue effect (Freud's term) is incorporation of material from the immediately preceding day. Nielsen and Powell proposed the "dream-lag effect" in 1989, a delayed reappearance of waking material roughly 5 to 7 days later (their 1989 title says 6 days; their 1992 limited replication supported day-residue clearly but was inconclusive for the lag) [23]. Nielsen, Kuiken, Alain, Stenstrom and Powell (2004) replicated both [48]. Blagrove et al. (2011a) had participants keep a daily diary and a dream diary for 14 days and rate every dream against every diary day, finding a significant lag for days 5-7 versus baseline and versus days 2-4, with future-event matching and same-weekday matching as controls [24]. Blagrove et al. (2011b, PLoS ONE) found the lag for laboratory REM but not N2 dreams in 20 participants, interpreting it as "a 7-day sleep-dependent non-linear memory consolidation process that is specific to REM sleep" [24a]. For an app that also keeps a short day-log, this supports two features: show "possible sources" from the previous day and from 5 to 7 days earlier when a tag reappears, and treat weekday periodicity as a confound to be controlled (Blagrove compared against the same weekday one week before and after).

### 3. Dream recall

#### 3.1 Population baseline

Schredl (2008) analysed a representative German sample of 931 persons over 14: age, sex and size of place of residence had small but significant effects (women and big-city residents recall more; recall declines with age), with small explained variance [25]. The abstract does not publish the category-by-category distribution, so no population histogram is asserted here (not verified from primary text). On the same Schredl scale (never / less than once a month / about once a month / 2-3 times a month / once a week / several times a week / almost every morning) the companion study found a correlation of .57 between dream recall frequency and lucid dream frequency, and 51% of 919 German adults reported at least one lucid dream in their life [26]. Saunders, Roe, Smith and Clegg's meta-analysis of 50 years of studies estimates lifetime lucid-dream prevalence at 55% (95% CI 49-62%, 34 studies) and monthly-or-more frequency at 23% (95% CI 20-25%, 25 studies) [27].

#### 3.2 Factors

Schredl, Wittmann, Ciric and Götz (2003) tested a structural equation model in 444 participants: personality (openness to experience, thin boundaries, absorption), creativity, nocturnal awakenings and attitude toward dreams were significant but together explained only 8.4% of variance; each factor alone under 3% [28]. The most robust factor is awakening from REM: Nielsen's review of 35 laboratory awakening studies gives mean recall of 81.9% from REM versus 43% from NREM [29], and a 2025 review of 69 further studies confirms awakening method (alarms reduce recall relative to a spoken name), multi-day protocols and environment all matter [29a]. Schredl's 2003 abstract explicitly names sleep duration, introspection and cognitive functioning immediately on waking (sleep inertia) as unmeasured candidates [28]. Attitude measures only correlate with recall when they refer to one's own recall, so Schredl recommends measuring attitude and recall independently [28a].

#### 3.3 Techniques with the best support

From LaBerge's chapter on recall [1] and the course's "Dream Recall Aids" [2], all of which are built on the REM-awakening evidence:

- Extra sleep: "It is more effective for increasing dream recall to sleep later in the morning than to go to bed earlier at night" because REM periods lengthen later in the night ("The first dream of the night is the shortest, perhaps only ten minutes ... after eight hours of sleep, dream periods can be forty-five minutes to an hour long") [1][2].
- Setting intention: write "I will remember my dreams" in the journal before bed; tell yourself you will have interesting dreams [1][2].
- A visible reminder by the bed [2].
- Asking the question on waking: "ask yourself this question the moment you awaken: 'What was I dreaming?' Do this first ... Don't move from the position in which you awaken, as any body movement may make your dream harder to remember. Also, don't think of the day's concerns" [1]. If nothing comes, ask "What was I just thinking?" and "How was I just feeling?", then rebuild backwards: "When you recall a scene, ask yourself what happened before that, and before that, reliving the dream in reverse" [1].
- Alarm at REM-rich times: "REM periods occur about every 90 minutes ... good times to set an alarm for are 4.5, 6 and 7.5 hours after you go to sleep" [1][2].
- Write immediately: "Don't wait until you get up in the morning ... be sure to write down at least a few key words about the dream immediately upon awakening" [1]. The course adds: note the time, key events, feelings and "any verbatim speech or text", then expand in the morning [2].
- Keep the journal by the bed with a pen, light and clock [2].

Evidence that journaling itself raises recall: Schredl (2002) found in 285 participants over two weeks that a diary increased recall in low and medium recallers but decreased it in high recallers [30]; a 2025 four-week diary study of 81 participants reported the expected logbook enhancement effect [30a]. LaBerge summarises the practical experience: "As you record more dreams, you will remember more" [1]. Voice memos and keyword anchors are common app features (Awoken, Lucidity, Oniri, MILD Trainer all offer speech-to-text) but no controlled study comparing voice to written capture was found; the "staying still" instruction is likewise based on LaBerge's clinical advice rather than a trial. Garfield's advice is similar in spirit: in an interview she says recall is a learned skill and suggests, if you wake with nothing, to "gently roll into the other sleep position and lie there for a moment" [42].

#### 3.4 How many dreams per night

Sleep cycles alternate NREM and REM about every 90 minutes (70 to 120 minutes), giving four to six cycles per night with REM episodes lengthening across the night [31]. LaBerge adds that "You may have more than one dream during a REM (dream) period, separated by short arousals that are most often forgotten", and that sleep researchers generally accept dreams are recalled only when the sleeper wakes directly from them [1]. In Aspy's 2020 study, participants recalled on average 1.7 dreams per night on ordinary nights and 2.8 on nights with a lucid dream [33]. The course sets the training targets: at least one dream per night for seven nights in a row, then aim for two per night, counting any fragment as one dream [2].

#### 3.5 Recall and lucid-dream induction

In the National Australian Lucid Dream Induction Study (Aspy, Delfabbro, Proeve and Mohr, 2017; N = 169, one baseline week then one practice week), the group combining reality testing, wake-back-to-bed and MILD had lucid dreams on 17.4% of nights in week 2 (up from 9.4%); lucid dreaming occurred on 45.8% of occasions when the person fell asleep within five minutes of finishing MILD; higher general dream recall significantly predicted MILD success, but the strongest predictor was time to fall back asleep [32][33]. The International Lucid Dream Induction Study (Aspy 2020) replicated MILD at a weighted 16.5% of nights and SSILD at 16.9%, found the five-minute effect weaker (ten minutes: 18.3% vs 11.1%, significant), found week-2 lucid dreaming correlated with general dream recall, and found daily reality-test counts uncorrelated with lucid dreaming ("RT appears to be an ineffective lucid dream induction technique" over one week) [33]. These are exactly the variables an app should log per night: technique(s) used, estimated time to fall back asleep after the technique, dreams recalled, lucid or not.

### 4. Journaling best practices and entry schema

#### 4.1 What the sources actually say

- LaBerge and Rheingold [1]: dedicate a notebook; keep it by the bed; record immediately or jot key words; describe how images "look and sound and smell" and "the way you felt in the dream - emotional reactions are important clues"; record anything unusual; sketch images; date at the top; title each complete dream with "a short, catchy title" ("The Guardian of the Spring", "Riot in the Classroom"); record fragments "no matter how unimportant"; leave a blank page after each dream for later exercises (dreamsign marking).
- Levitan and LaBerge course [2]: each night write the date and bedtime; at each awakening write the time and notes; in the morning write the full dream and title it; the Dream Recall Log records per night: date, bedtime, a tick per hour-after-bedtime slot in which a dream was recalled, dream count, lucid-dream count, and which recall aids were used (extra sleep, intention, reminder, asking the question, alarm, DreamAlarm). The dreamsign forms add: dreamsign text, category, DSA rating 0-4, and waking-life dreamsigns.
- Tholey [4]: the value of testing memory of the preceding period and of noting what triggered recognition (he recognised his first lucid dream via a dead relative, a classic "character place" sign).
- Waggoner (*Lucid Dreaming: Gateway to the Inner Self*, 2008) discusses "levels of lucidity" and five stages of lucid dreaming development in his workshops and book, but a specific 0-5 numeric scale attributed to him could not be verified from any retrievable text (not verified) [41].
- Garfield (*Creative Dreaming*, 1974/1995) has a chapter on keeping a dream diary; only her interview remarks on recall were retrievable [42].
- Daniel Love (*Are You Dreaming?*, 2013), Charlie Morley (*Lucid Dreaming: A Beginner's Guide*, 2013 / *Dreams of Awakening*) and Thomas Yuschak (*Advanced Lucid Dreaming: The Power of Supplements*, 2006) all recommend a bedside journal, pre-sleep intention and reviewing entries for dream signs according to reviews and summaries [43][44][45]; their exact field lists and any rating scales could not be verified from primary text and are not attributed here.

#### 4.3 Rating scales in use

- Dreamsign Awareness Scale 0-4 (Lucidity Institute) [2]: the only LaBerge-group scale found in primary text; works per dream sign.
- LuCiD scale (Voss, Schermelleh-Engel, Windt, Frenzel and Hobson 2013): 28 items in eight factors, INSIGHT, CONTROL, THOUGHT, REALISM, MEMORY, DISSOCIATION, NEGATIVE EMOTION, POSITIVE EMOTION; realism and negative emotion did not differentiate lucid from non-lucid dreams [35]. Too long for daily use but a good source for sub-scale wording (insight, control, memory).
- Dream Lucidity Questionnaire (DLQ): Aspy's studies use a lucidity questionnaire, and other authors cite a DLQ by Stumbrys, Erlacher and Schredl (2013), but the item count ("12 items") could not be verified from an accessible text [33] (not verified).
- Simple Likert awareness scale 0-4 ("not at all" to "very much") used in recent research, with mid-values treated as "semi-lucid" [41a].
- Aspy's Dream Quantity measure: fragmentary 1, partial 2, majority 4, whole 8, summed per night [33]. A clean, research-backed "recall clarity" scale.

Community "level of lucidity" ladders (1-5, "low/medium/high", "layers") exist on forums but are explicitly non-standard [41a]. Recommendation: lucid yes/no plus DSA 0-4 per sign plus 1-5 sliders for awareness, control and vividness, with the LuCiD factor names as tooltips.

### 5. Analysis features a journal can compute

#### 5.1 Core metrics (all computable from tagged entries)

- Dream-sign frequency: count of dreams containing each sign, split by LaBerge category; the book's own "rank categories by frequency, pick the top" is the first analysis to ship [1].
- Personal dream-sign ranking: frequency weighted by effect. The self-hosted project drem computes, for each sign, the lucid rate of dreams containing it versus the archive baseline [40c]; combining frequency with mean DSA rating (how often the sign is noticed as odd) matches the course's logic that awareness of strangeness predicts lucidity [2].
- Co-occurrence: sign x sign and character x setting matrices; DreamBank-style word strings are the research precedent [11].
- Trends: rolling 7- and 28-day recall rate (dreams per night, nights with at least one dream), lucid rate per night (Aspy's metric: lucid nights / nights) and per dream, DSA distribution over time [2][33].
- HVDC-style frequencies: characters (familiar vs stranger, male vs female), settings (indoor/outdoor, familiar), aggression/friendliness, misfortune/good fortune, success/failure, emotion classes, compared against the norms in section 2.2 [9].
- Typical-theme frequencies against TDQ population prevalence [13].
- Word frequency and TF-IDF of nouns over time; "new words this month".
- Technique effectiveness: for each technique (and combination), lucid-night rate on nights used vs not used, with counts shown; also time-to-fall-asleep buckets (under 5, 5-10, over 10 minutes) because that was the strongest predictor [32][33].
- Sleep-time correlations: recall and lucidity versus total sleep, wake time, WBTB duration; extra morning sleep is the course's single "highly effective" recall aid [2].
- Reality-check "hit rate": reminders delivered vs acknowledged, and reminders tied to habitual moments vs random. Honest framing: Aspy 2020 found no correlation between daily RT count and lucid dreaming [33], so present hit rate as adherence, not as a predictor.
- Nightmare / bad-dream frequency from logs, which research shows is more accurate than retrospective estimates [18].
- Weekday patterns: recall differences by weekday are plausible because of weekend sleep length [2]; content periodicity by weekday is a confound in dream-lag research, not an established effect [24].
- Moon phase: Schredl, Fulda and Reinhard (2006) analysed diaries from 196 participants over 28 to 111 nights and found no association between full moon and dream recall; Cordi et al. (2014) found no lunar effect in 1,265 volunteers over 2,097 laboratory nights of sleep [36][49]. If the feature is offered, label it as "for curiosity, no evidence".

#### 5.2 Statistical cautions to build in

Dream series are autocorrelated and most users will have small N; Domhoff and Schneider apply multiple-comparison corrections and autocorrelation checks to long series [12a]. Show counts next to every percentage, suppress comparisons under about 20 dreams, and prefer "nights with technique X: 8 lucid of 40" over bare rates.

#### 5.3 Automated tagging that runs locally

Constraints: no cloud; all models open source and offline.

- Lexicon / keyword matching for dream signs: maintain per-user tag synonyms (e.g. "teeth", "tooth", "molar") and a seed lexicon built from the 55 TDQ items [13] and the LaBerge sub-types [1]. Match on lemmas. This is what Awoken's "Dream Pattern Analyzer" (hashtag and word themes) and Lucidity's automatic "dream dictionary" do at the product level [39a][39b].
- Named-entity and noun extraction: spaCy (MIT licence) small models can run on a server CPU and give PERSON, GPE/LOC, ORG entities plus noun chunks for candidate characters, settings and objects; capitalised unknown tokens are good "character" candidates for the user to confirm.
- HVDC categories as taxonomy: use the five HVDC emotion classes, the setting codes (indoor/outdoor, familiar/unfamiliar/distorted) and the character identity codes (family, known, stranger, prominent, dead, imaginary) as the fixed vocabularies behind the tag picker [9a]. Automatic HVDC coding is an active research area (sequence-to-sequence models for characters and emotions, LLM scoring of emotion), but those papers rely on large models; rule-based pre-filling with human confirmation is realistic locally.
- Emotion / sentiment lexicons and licensing: the NRC Word-Emotion Association Lexicon (EmoLex) has 14,182 words tagged for eight emotions and two sentiments, is free for non-commercial research and educational use, requires citation, prohibits redistribution of the data, and needs a commercial licence from NRC for commercial use [37]. For a product that is distributed, the safer defaults are VADER (MIT licence) for valence [38] and a project-owned emotion word list mapped to the five HVDC classes. Remember Sikka's finding: text-derived emotion under-counts positive affect, so always prefer the user's own rating when present [21].
- Local embeddings: drem uses Ollama with embeddinggemma for semantic search and lets local or remote models be assigned per task [40c]; the same pattern (optional local LLM, nothing leaves the host) is the right architecture for "similar dreams" and "suggested tags".
- Comparison corpus: DreamBank series may be used non-commercially with attribution (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) [10].

### 7. Reality-check reminder design

Intervals and designs in use: LaBerge's "five to ten different occasions during the day" chosen in advance, plus whenever something surprising, unlikely or emotionally intense happens [1]; Tholey's "several times" to five-to-ten times a day [3][4]; apps default to roughly 5-10 per day within a user-defined waking window (Awoken), fixed interval or random frequency (Oniri), custom times and time brackets (HowToLucid), and interval-based alerts (Lucidity iOS) [39a][39e][39o][39d]. Users specifically ask for random timing limited to waking hours, quiet hours, and vibration-only delivery [39o][39p]. LaBerge's "reflection-intention" variant adds habit anchors ("when you arrive home from work, at the beginning of each conversation you have, every hour on the hour") and visualisation of the anchor [1]; the dream-sign-linked anchors (elevator, boss, typo, bathroom) are the same idea [1].

Recommended checks and why:

- Re-read text or a digital clock, look away, look again. LaBerge: "Read some text twice. Don't conclude that you are awake unless you have solid proof (for example, the writing stays the same every time you look at it)" [1]; written text tends to change on second inspection in dreams (LaBerge and Rheingold 1990, as cited in the induction literature) [33].
- Memory test: "Think back to the events of the last several minutes. Do you have any trouble remembering what just happened? If so, you may be dreaming" [1]; Tholey calls recalling the preceding period "one of the most effective tests" [4].
- Light switch: Tholey recommends turning a light on after any apparent awakening because the memory test fails against false awakenings [4].
- Tholey's physical test: turn 180 degrees and try to stand still [4].
- Nose-pinch breathing, finger through palm, counting fingers, mirrors: widespread community checks (ld4all, Dreamviews, app tutorials) with no primary study of their individual reliability located; include them as options but do not claim evidence.
- Always pair the question with a test and with the imagination step: "After having satisfied yourself that you're awake, tell yourself, 'Okay, I'm not dreaming, now. But if I were, what would it be like?'" [1].

Evidence quality: Stumbrys, Erlacher, Schädlich and Schredl's 2012 systematic review (35 studies, 11 laboratory) found methodological quality "relatively low" and "none of the induction techniques were verified to induce lucid dreams reliably and consistently, although some of them look promising"; reality testing had mixed results across field studies [34]. Aspy 2017 found RT ineffective alone; Aspy 2020 found no correlation between RT count and lucid dreams over one week and a non-significant trend for RT groups to do worse (12.1% vs 19.4%) [32][33]. MILD and SSILD, combined with WBTB, are the techniques with the best replicated effect (about 16-20% of nights after one week of practice) [33]. The honest app framing is: reminders train the habit that MILD and Tholey's technique rely on, adherence is measurable, but the lucid-dream payoff is documented for MILD/WBTB and the time-to-sleep variable, not for reminder counts.

# Part D: Techniques, in-dream skills, traditions and training programmes


Research note for a self-hosted lucid-dreaming journal and training app. Compiled 2026-11-06. Every technique below is attributed to its originator and labelled with an evidence level:

- **Controlled study**: tested in a randomised or sleep-laboratory study with a comparison condition.
- **Field study / survey**: tested with logbooks or questionnaires, usually self-selected participants, no lab verification.
- **Case / self-experiment**: reported by the originator from his or her own practice (for example LaBerge's dissertation record).
- **Anecdotal (community)**: originated on a forum (DreamViews, LD4all, Reddit) and supported only by user reports.
- **Tradition**: a contemplative lineage practice; presented as the tradition describes it, not as a scientific claim.

Where a step list is quoted or closely paraphrased from a source, the reference number follows in brackets. Where I could not verify a detail against a primary source, I say so in the text. A verification summary is at the end.

### 1. Dream-initiated techniques (DILD family)

A DILD (dream-initiated lucid dream) is one in which you realise you are dreaming from inside an ongoing dream, usually by noticing a dreamsign or by habitually questioning your state [1, ch. 3]. The techniques below all try to carry a waking habit of critical reflection or a prospective intention into the dream.

#### 1.1 MILD - Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams (LaBerge)

- **Origin**: Stephen LaBerge, Stanford, developed during his doctoral work and published as a case study in 1980 [2]; revised for the public in *Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming* (EWLD, 1990) [1]. LaBerge reports that before induction procedures he recalled fewer than one lucid dream a month; with autosuggestion about five a month; with MILD "as many as four lucid dreams in one night and as many as twenty-six in one month", and one or more lucid dreams on twenty of twenty-one laboratory nights [1, ch. 3].
- **Evidence level**: Case / self-experiment (LaBerge), then controlled field studies: NALDIS 2017 [11] and ILDIS 2020 [12], and sleep-laboratory studies by Erlacher and Stumbrys [13, 14]. Among cognitive techniques it has the most consistent supporting evidence [15].
- **Prerequisite (LaBerge)**: good dream recall ("you should recall at least one dream per night") and a demonstrated ability to remember intentions while awake; LaBerge provides a one-week "prospective memory training" exercise for this (see 1.6) [1, ch. 3].

**Steps as LaBerge gives them in EWLD** [1, ch. 3, "MILD technique"]:

1. **Set up dream recall.** Before bed, resolve to wake up and recall dreams during each dream period of the night (or the first dream period after dawn, or whenever convenient).
2. **Recall your dream.** When you wake from a dream, recall as many details as possible. If you are so drowsy you are drifting back to sleep, do something to arouse yourself.
3. **Focus your intent.** While returning to sleep, concentrate single-mindedly on your intention to remember to recognise that you are dreaming. Tell yourself: "Next time I'm dreaming, I want to remember I'm dreaming." Really try to feel that you mean it. If you find yourself thinking of anything else, let the thought go and return to the intention.
4. **See yourself becoming lucid.** At the same time, imagine you are back in the dream you just woke from, but this time you recognise it is a dream. Find a dreamsign in the experience; when you see it, say to yourself "I'm dreaming!" and continue the fantasy, for example imagining yourself taking off and flying.
5. **Repeat.** Repeat steps 3 and 4 until your intention is set, then let yourself fall asleep. If you find yourself thinking of anything else while falling asleep, repeat the procedure so that the last thing in your mind before sleep is the intention to remember to recognise the next time you are dreaming.

LaBerge's commentary: if it takes a long time to fall asleep, do not worry - the longer you are awake, the more times you repeat the procedure and the more alert your brain becomes. "If you are a very deep sleeper, you should get up after memorizing your dream and engage in ten to fifteen minutes of any activity requiring full wakefulness"; one of the best is to write out the dream and read it over, noting dreamsigns, in preparation for the MILD visualisation [1, ch. 3]. The Lucidity Institute FAQ gives the same four core steps with the phrasing "Next time I'm dreaming, I will remember I'm dreaming," repeated "like a mantra", and states that the proper time to practise MILD is after awakening from a dream, before returning to sleep [3].

**The Aspy 2017 (NALDIS) version** [11]. Participants in the reality-testing + WBTB + MILD group were told to:

1. Set an alarm for five hours after going to bed.
2. When it goes off, put a light on, sit upright in bed and try to remember a dream from just before waking (or any recent dream).
3. Go to the bathroom if necessary, turn off the light, lie down comfortably and repeat the phrase "next time I'm dreaming, I will remember that I'm dreaming", putting meaning into the words.
4. Simultaneously imagine being back in the recalled dream and noticing something unusual or bizarre that makes you realise you are dreaming.
5. Repeat until you fall asleep or your intention is set; if your mind wanders, repeat so that the last thought before sleep is the intention. Participants were told the longer they spent on the technique the more effective it would be, and not to worry if it took a long time to fall asleep.

Results: in the RT + WBTB + MILD group (n = 47), lucid dreams rose from 9.4% of nights in the baseline week to 17.4% in the technique week; the RT-only group showed no increase. The much-quoted "five-minute rule" is a post-hoc finding, not an instruction: attempts in which participants fell asleep within five minutes of finishing MILD had a success rate of about 46%, and falling asleep within five minutes was associated with an 86% increase in lucid-dream frequency [11]. In the larger ILDIS replication (N = 355), falling asleep within 10 minutes of finishing the technique significantly predicted success (18.3% vs 11.1% of nights), whereas the 5-minute cut-off did not reach significance; MILD + WBTB produced lucid dreams on 16.5-20% of nights, and general dream recall was a predictor of success [12].

**When to do it**: on waking from a dream in the last third of the night, or after a planned WBTB awakening at about 4.5-6 hours [11, 12, 13]. The Course in Lucid Dreaming also teaches a "bedtime" MILD and warns that if you concentrate for more than 20 minutes without falling asleep you may let go, relax and simply keep the intention as your last thought [5, Unit 3].

**Common errors**: repeating the phrase mechanically without visualising; trying MILD before dream recall is reliable (LaBerge's stated prerequisite); staying awake so long that you cannot return to sleep (ILDIS participants with insomnia were told to keep the lights off and use a quieter alarm) [12]; treating "five minutes" as a target to rush rather than a correlate.

#### 1.2 Reality testing / critical state testing (LaBerge, after Tholey)

- **Origin**: Paul Tholey's "reflection technique" (1959) is the root [7, 8]; LaBerge's "critical state testing" and "reflection-intention" techniques are explicit adaptations of Tholey [1, ch. 3]. The specific tests (reading twice, digital watch, trying to fly) come from LaBerge [1, 3].
- **Evidence level**: Field studies. Reality testing on its own did not increase lucid dreaming in one week in NALDIS [11] or ILDIS, where the author concludes "RT appears to be an ineffective lucid dream induction technique - at least for short periods such as one week" [12]. Tholey and LaBerge both treat it as most useful for beginners and in combination with intention [1, 8].

**Critical state testing as LaBerge gives it** [1, ch. 3]:

1. **Plan when to test your state.** Pick five to ten occasions during the day that resemble your dreams (contact with a dreamsign; anything surprising, unlikely, emotionally intense or dreamlike; recurrent-dream situations). LaBerge's example list: stepping into an elevator, speaking to the boss, seeing an attractive woman, reading a typographical error, going to the bathroom.
2. **Test your state.** Ask "Am I dreaming or awake?" - "Don't just automatically ask the question and mindlessly reply, 'Obviously, I'm awake,' or you will do the same thing when you actually are dreaming." Look for oddities; think back over the last several minutes and check whether you have trouble remembering what just happened.

**Which tests, and why** [1, ch. 3; 3]:

- *Read text twice*: find writing, read it, look away, read it again. "Every time I have tried this in my own lucid dreams the writing has mutated in some way." The Lucidity Institute states that text in dreams changes 75% of the time when re-read once and 95% when re-read twice [3]. LaBerge calls this the best test and advises using the same test each time.
- *Digital watch or clock*: look at it twice; "in a dream, it will never behave correctly". Analog watches can tell plausible dream time, so they are not reliable. Beware of rationalisations ("the battery is low").
- *Try to fly*: hop and try to prolong time off the ground; even a split second longer than normal means you are dreaming.
- *Pinching yourself* is explicitly rejected: it just produces a convincing pinch.
- *Nose-pinch breathing* (close the lips, pinch the nose, try to inhale) and *finger through palm* are the two tests used in Aspy's studies [11, 12]; they come from community practice and VILD/FILD (see 2.4, 2.5), not from LaBerge.
- *Light switch*: Tholey recommends, on any apparent awakening, turning a light on; if it does not go on, suspect a dream [8].
- *Memory test* (Tholey): try to remember what preceded the present situation; gaps or bizarre events indicate a dream. Tholey calls this one of the most effective tests, but warns it is useless after a false awakening, hence the light-switch check [7, 8].
- *Turning test* (Tholey): turn 180 degrees and try to stand still; in a dream the body keeps turning or the surroundings revolve the other way [7, 8].
- *Counting fingers* and *jumping* are widely used community checks; I found no originator and they should be labelled community practice.

**Why with a genuine question**: all three sources insist on it. Tholey's whole method rests on building a "critical-reflective attitude"; he notes that with practice "the posing of critical questions [becomes] a superfluous matter" because the disposition itself does the work [8]. KingYoshi's ADA tutorial says the same: "A quality RC is a RC in which you question your reality" [20]. LaBerge's "last word in state testing": "Anytime you find yourself seriously suspecting that you just might be dreaming, you probably are!" [1, ch. 3].

**How many per day**: LaBerge: at least the five to ten selected occasions, "a dozen times or more" in the reflection-intention version [1]; Tholey: "as often as possible", at least five to ten times a day, in dreamlike situations, and ideally just before falling asleep, especially in the early morning hours [1, 8]; Aspy: a minimum of 10 per day, at a range of times, especially when something unusual happens [11, 12]; the Lucidity Institute course assigns eight planned occasions per day [5, Unit 2].

#### 1.3 Tholey's reflection technique and combined technique

- **Origin**: Paul Tholey, Frankfurt, first used 1959; published in English in "Techniques for inducing and manipulating lucid dreams", *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 57, 79-90 (1983), based on "research conducted over several years and with over 200 subjects" [6]; described in his own words in *Lucidity Letter* 1988 and 1989 [7, 8].
- **Evidence level**: Tholey's own multi-subject phenomenological studies; later independent tests found it less effective than MILD. Aspy 2020 cites Kumar et al. (2018) as finding lucid dreams on at most 6% of days with Tholey's combined technique [12]. Tholey himself claimed that "subjects who have never previously experienced a lucid dream will have the first one after a median time of 4 to 5 [weeks]" and that most subjects who consistently follow the advice "experience at least one lucid dream every night" (quoted in [1, ch. 3]); treat this as an originator's claim.

**Reflection technique in Tholey's words** [8]: "the subject asks him or herself several times during the course of the day: 'Am I awake, or am I dreaming?' The purpose is to achieve a generally critical attitude towards one's state of consciousness." Tholey had his first lucid dream after four weeks.

**Three factors Tholey found decisive for the critical question** [7]: ask it (1) as often as possible, (2) as much as possible in situations similar to dream experiences, and (3) ideally just before falling asleep, especially in the early morning hours. "The experienced lucid dreamer does not need to ask the critical question during the day."

**Combined technique**: Tholey states that the reflection technique was expanded "by incorporating elements of intention and auto-suggestion" (Tholey 1982, 1983) [8]. The complete step list from the 1983 paper was not available to me in full text. LaBerge's published adaptation, explicitly marked "Adapted from Tholey", is the following "Reflection-Intention Technique" [1, ch. 3]:

1. **Plan when you intend to test your state** (a dozen or more times a day at chosen occasions, plus whenever anything is surprising, odd, emotionally strong, or your memory feels unresponsive). Use imagery to remember to ask the question.
2. **Test your state**: ask "Am I dreaming or awake?", look for oddities, think back over the last few minutes, read some text twice. Do not conclude you are awake without solid proof.
3. **Imagine yourself dreaming**: having satisfied yourself you are awake, say "Okay, I'm not dreaming, now. But if I were, what would it be like?" Imagine as vividly as possible that everything you perceive is a dream; look for your target dreamsigns; then say "The next time I'm dreaming, I will remember to recognize that I'm dreaming."
4. **Imagine doing what you intend to do in your lucid dream**: decide in advance on an action, continue the fantasy and see yourself carrying it out after becoming lucid. Firmly resolve to recognise that you are dreaming and do the intended action.

A community note: the critical question should also prompt an attempt to recall what happened just before, which is Tholey's memory test [7]. The Lucidity Institute course assigns eight occasions per day for this exercise and asks students to log hits [5, Unit 2].

#### 1.4 Intention technique (Tholey, adapted by LaBerge) and the Tibetan "power of resolution"

- **Origin**: Tibetan dream yoga ("comprehending it by the power of resolution", Evans-Wentz 1935 [35]); Tholey's intention technique; LaBerge's adaptation [1, ch. 3].
- **Evidence level**: Tradition; Tholey's studies; LaBerge's self-report.

**Power of resolution (LaBerge's distillation of the Tibetan text)** [1, ch. 3]: (1) Day practice: "under all conditions" think continuously that "all things are of the substance of dreams" and resolve to realise their true nature. (2) Night practice: when about to sleep, "firmly resolve" that you will comprehend the dream state. LaBerge adds the Tibetan note that it takes "no fewer than twenty-one efforts each morning".

**Intention technique (LaBerge's adaptation of Tholey)** [1, ch. 3]:

1. **Resolve to recognise dreaming**: in the early morning hours or during a late-night awakening, clearly and confidently affirm your intention to remember to recognise the dream state.
2. **Visualise yourself recognising dreaming**: imagine as vividly as possible being in dream situations that would typically cause you to realise you are dreaming, using your most frequent dreamsigns.
3. **Imagine carrying out an intended dream action**: resolve to do a chosen action in the dream, ideally one that is itself a dreamsign (for example flying). Rationale: dreamers sometimes do the action before becoming lucid and then realise "This is what I wanted to do in my dream. Therefore, I must be dreaming!"

#### 1.5 Autosuggestion (Garfield, LaBerge, Tholey) and post-hypnotic suggestion

- **Origin**: Patricia Garfield (1975) reported increasing prolonged lucid dreams from zero to about three per week with self-suggestion [1, ch. 3]; LaBerge used it for the first sixteen months of his dissertation (5.4 lucid dreams per month); Tholey used it as a component of the combined technique and recommends suggestions "immediately before sleep, while in a relaxed state", cautioning that "an effort of will must be avoided" [1, ch. 3].
- **Evidence level**: Case / self-experiment. LaBerge: effortful intention (MILD) was far more reliable than non-effortful suggestion (one of six lab nights vs twenty of twenty-one).

**Autosuggestion technique (LaBerge)** [1, ch. 3]: (1) Relax completely in bed (progressive relaxation if needed). (2) While deeply relaxed, suggest to yourself that you are going to have a lucid dream, tonight or soon. "Avoid putting intentional effort into your suggestion. Do not strongly insist with statements like 'Tonight I will have a lucid dream!'" Instead cultivate genuine expectation. The Course in Lucid Dreaming calls it "a low-key, pressure-free method to use when you are feeling low on powers of mental concentration" [5].

**Post-hypnotic suggestion (PHS)**: LaBerge tried PHS on three occasions and succeeded twice; he cites Joseph Dane's dissertation in which fifteen women who had never had lucid dreams developed a personal dream symbol under hypnosis and then visualised it while asking for a lucid dream; they reported longer, more involving lucid dreams in the lab than hypnotised controls [1, ch. 3]. Evidence: one small controlled study, not replicated to my knowledge.

#### 1.6 Prospective memory training (LaBerge)

- **Origin**: LaBerge, EWLD, as a stated prerequisite for MILD; extended as the "I Remember" game in the Lucidity Institute course [1, ch. 3; 5, Unit 3].
- **Evidence level**: Rationale from prospective-memory research; no isolated test.

**Exercise** [1, ch. 3]: (1) Each morning read only that day's four target events (for example Sunday: next time I see a pet or animal; look at my face in a mirror; turn on a light; see a flower) and memorise them. (2) During the day, when you notice the *next* occurrence of each target, perform a state test ("Am I dreaming?"). (3) At the end of the day record how many of the four targets you hit; a target noticed only on a later occurrence counts as a miss. (4) Continue for at least a week; if you still miss most targets, continue with your own lists until you hit most of them.

#### 1.7 Dreamsign targeting (LaBerge)

- **Origin**: LaBerge coined "dreamsign"; the inventory and cataloguing exercise are in EWLD chapter 2 and the Lucidity Institute course Unit 1 [1, 3, 5].
- **Evidence level**: Component of MILD and of the DreamLight studies; no isolated controlled test.

**Dreamsign inventory** [1, ch. 2]: four categories - *inner awareness* (peculiar thoughts, inappropriate or overwhelming emotion, unusual sensations such as paralysis or leaving the body, altered perception), *action* (impossible or odd actions by you, others, objects), *form* (bizarre or transforming shapes of people, places, things), *context* (odd combinations, being somewhere unlikely, people out of place, playing an unaccustomed role).

**Cataloguing your dreamsigns** [1, ch. 2]: (1) Keep a dream journal; collect at least a dozen dreams. (2) Mark dreamsigns in each report (underline, then list). (3) Classify each by category. (4) Count categories and rank; the most frequent is your target category. (5) Practise looking for that category in waking life so you know how things usually are and will notice when something is off. The Course adds a "Waking Life Dreamsigns" list and the instruction to tell yourself that when you next see your dreamsigns you will recognise them as cues [5, Units 1-3]. This is what "lucid dream induction via dream signs" means in the LaBerge literature; the same logic is applied to artificial dreamsigns (light or sound cues from a device) [3].

#### 1.8 All Day Awareness - ADA (KingYoshi, DreamViews)

- **Origin**: Posted by KingYoshi on DreamViews, 28 March 2011, "All Day Awareness, A DILD Tutorial"; he credits the basic concept to DreamViews member Naiya and says he coined the term and acronym [20].
- **Evidence level**: Anecdotal (community). KingYoshi's own claim: 107 DILDs logged on the site, 150+ in total, "hovering around the 22 lucid dreams per month mark", about 90% of them from awareness rather than a reality check; the thread collects many user success reports. No controlled study. The idea is close to Tholey's observation that the questioning habit matures into a "passively receptive focus on current experience" [8] and to the mindfulness-lucidity correlation found in surveys [18].

**The practice as KingYoshi describes it** [20]:

1. ADA "is noticing and paying attention to the subtle things in life that most people ignore or take for granted": footsteps, the feel of shoes, muscles working, blinking, breathing, smells and whether they change, shadows of every structure, the keys under your fingers, the clothes on your body.
2. "At first, you will have to force yourself to be completely aware of your surroundings"; with practice it becomes natural and "the dream itself becomes your dream sign".
3. **Getting started** (added after reader feedback): start small. Pick several times a day when you are not busy and spend 5-10 minutes noticing everything you can; do some reality checks in those sessions; increase session frequency as it gets easier. "Be sure not to overwork yourself. If you start feeling mentally exhausted, take a day off."
4. **Reality checks are for confirming, not creating, lucidity.** Before each RC, become aware of your surroundings and "pretend that everything around you is actually a dream", then test.
5. **Dreamsigns** still matter as recurring elements that boost familiarity with the dream world.
6. **Journal** every dream; lie still and recap before writing; write "Couldn't remember any dreams" if nothing comes. KingYoshi's recall aid: alarms after 4 h, 6 h and then hourly.

**Common errors** (from the thread): treating ADA as a mechanical "Am I dreaming?" check; trying to sustain it all day from the start, causing stress; neglecting the journal.

#### 1.9 Castaneda's "look at your hands"

- **Origin**: Carlos Castaneda, *Journey to Ixtlan* (1972): don Juan tells Castaneda "Tonight in your dreams you must look at your hands", the hands being chosen because they are always available; when the scene wavers, look back at the hands to steady it; later books (*The Art of Dreaming*, 1993) call finding the hands "the first gate of dreaming" [25]. Castaneda's books are presented as non-fiction but are widely regarded as fictional; the technique is nonetheless used by many practitioners, and both Sparrow [38] and Waggoner [26] report it as the route to early lucid dreams.
- **Evidence level**: Anecdotal; Sparrow's argument (quoted by LaBerge) is that the dreamer's own body is one of the most stable reference points in a dream, so looking at it stabilises the scene [1, ch. 6].

**Practice (community form)**: during the day, look at your hands and ask whether you are dreaming; before sleep, resolve to find your hands in the dream; in the dream, finding the hands triggers lucidity and looking back at them stabilises it [25, 1].

#### 1.10 Cycle Adjustment Technique - CAT (Daniel Love)

- **Origin**: Daniel Love, UK; text marked "(c) 2004 Daniel Love", circulated on LD4all and later printed in full in *Are You Dreaming?* (2013) [24].
- **Evidence level**: Anecdotal (originator's reports). Love states that in his experience and with his students "8 of 10" reported at least one lucid dream (about two on average) within two weeks, that the method yields at best about four long clear lucid dreams a week, and that it "has not been researched widely enough for a definitive success rate" [24].

**Steps** [24]:

*Stage 1 - cycle adjustment, 7 days.* Keep a fixed bedtime and wake time. Set the alarm 90 minutes before your normal wake time. Each night set the intention to wake at the adjusted time. When the alarm goes off, get up and stay up. For the next 90 minutes do ordinary activities and perform a reality test every 2-5 minutes (a timer helps). Then carry on with the day; daytime reality tests are optional. Repeat every day for the week.

*Stage 2 - ongoing induction, alternating days.* Day A: set the intention to reality test at the earlier time, but set the alarm for your normal wake time; the body, expecting to wake early, is in a long REM period at that time; if you wake naturally at the early time, stay still and try to induce a lucid dream. Day B: repeat the Stage 1 routine (wake 90 minutes early, stay up, reality test for 90 minutes). Alternate A and B without missing days. If you stop, restart from Stage 1. Love says strict adherence is vital and suggests refreshing the cycle with Stage 1 every few months.

**Common errors**: sleeping through the early alarm; irregular schedules; disturbing a bed partner; sleep loss - Love's design deliberately trades sleep continuity for timing, so it is unsuitable for anyone who cannot keep the schedule.

### 2. Wake-initiated techniques (WILD family)

A WILD (wake-initiated lucid dream) is entered "directly from the waking state, without passing through other stages of sleep and with unbroken awareness"; preliminary research indicates WILDs are most common in the late morning and during morning or afternoon naps, when the drive to enter REM is strongest [5, Unit 4]. Tholey classifies these as "techniques allowing the subject to maintain lucidity ... while one is falling asleep" [7]. LaBerge groups them by what you attend to: hypnagogic imagery, a visualisation, a repetitive mental task, or the body [1, ch. 4].

#### 2.1 WILD basics: relaxation, hypnagogia, vibrations, sleep paralysis, entering the dream

**What to expect** [1, ch. 4; 5, Unit 4]: the physiological state is a waking, active brain with a body in REM paralysis. Reported sensations include being unable to move, weights on the chest, "electricity", vibrations, loud buzzing or roaring, body parts melting, and the feeling of leaving the body. "Sleep paralysis can be quite terrifying, but it is harmless." Panic is counterproductive: "such emotional panic reactions are completely counterproductive; they are likely to stimulate the limbic (emotional) areas of the brain and cause the REM state to persist." The advice is to relax, remember you are in the state you are in every night during REM, and treat it as the threshold of a dream: "Just step over and you're in the world of lucid dreams" [1, ch. 4].

**Relaxation** (LaBerge's preliminaries to every WILD exercise) [1, chs. 2, 4]: lie in bed, close the eyes, relax head, neck, back, arms and legs, breathe slowly; if just awakened you are probably already relaxed, otherwise use *progressive relaxation* or the *61-point relaxation* (passing attention through 61 body points, adapted from Swami Rama).

**Hypnagogic imagery technique (LaBerge)** [1, ch. 4]:

1. Relax completely.
2. Observe the visual images: focus gently on the images that appear before the mind's eye; watch how they begin and end; observe "as delicately as possible", without holding on, as a detached observer. Fleeting patterns gradually develop into scenes and extended sequences.
3. Enter the dream: when the imagery becomes a moving, vivid scenario, "allow yourself to be passively drawn into the dream world. Do not try to actively enter the dream scene." Be careful of "too much involvement and too little attention".

LaBerge quotes Tholey: "It is not desirable to want actively to enter into the scenery, since such an intention as a rule causes the scenery to disappear ... the subject should attempt to let himself be carried into it passively." Tholey also recommends resolving beforehand to carry out a particular action in the dream, so that if you lose lucidity on entry you can regain it [1, ch. 4].

**Count yourself to sleep technique (LaBerge, from his dissertation)** [1, ch. 4]: (1) Relax completely. (2) As you drift off, count "1, I'm dreaming; 2, I'm dreaming ...", maintaining a degree of vigilance; start over after 100 if you wish. (3) At some point you will be saying "I'm dreaming ..." and notice that you are. LaBerge adds that an assistant who wakes you at signs of sleep onset (slow pendular eye movements, twitches, irregular breathing) and asks what number you reached accelerates learning.

**Body-focused techniques (LaBerge, adapted from Tholey and Rama)** [1, ch. 4]:

- *Twin bodies*: after waking from a dream, lie on the back or right side; pass attention through the 61 points; watch for strange sensations, vibrations and body-image distortions, "the harbingers of REM sleep paralysis"; when paralysis is complete, imagine your "magical, move-able twin", the dream body, floating, rolling or sitting up out of the physical body. Then, LaBerge insists, do state tests - everything including the bed and the "sleeping body" is a dream.
- *One body*: the same, but understanding that there is only one body image; once sensory input is cut off, simply imagine the body image can move again and that you are somewhere else.
- *No body*: while falling asleep, concentrate on the thought that your body will become imperceptible; then float about the dream as a point of awareness (Tholey's "ego-point technique"); Tholey's *image-ego-point* variant adds hypnagogic imagery, and the ego-point "can under certain circumstances enter into the body of another dream figure and take over its 'motor system'".

**Tibetan visualisation WILDs as adapted by LaBerge** [1, ch. 4]: *White dot technique* (before bed resolve to recognise dreaming, visualise a radiant red "ah" in the throat; at dawn do "pot-shaped" breathing seven times, resolve eleven times to comprehend the dream state, then concentrate on a bone-white dot between the eyebrows until you find you are dreaming); *black dot technique* (meditate on the white dot before bed; at dawn 21 breaths, 21 resolutions, then a pill-sized black dot at the base of the genitals); *dream lotus and flame technique* (from Tarthang Tulku: visualise in the throat a pale-pink lotus with a reddish-orange flame, maintain it against all other imagery until image and awareness merge). LaBerge notes that if visualisation is difficult you should first practise candle concentration and visualisation training (EWLD appendix).

**Evidence level for the WILD family**: case reports and Tholey's phenomenological studies; no controlled induction trial isolates WILD. Erlacher and Stumbrys 2020 note that in their lab the induced lucid dreams after WBTB were mostly dream-initiated [13].

**Common errors**: forcing entry (Tholey: active wanting makes the scenery disappear); panicking at paralysis; trying WILD at bedtime, when the first REM period is short and far away [19]; practising so long that sleep is lost; moving on awakening (see 2.6).

#### 2.2 SSILD - Senses Initiated Lucid Dream (Cosmic Iron)

- **Origin**: Developed in 2011 by the author posting as Cosmic Iron on a Chinese lucid-dreaming forum (first version titled "太玄功", which he translates as "A Very Mysterious Technique"), refined collaboratively, then published in English about a year later; the "official tutorial 2.0" is dated 24 January 2013 and hosted on cosmiciron.blogspot.com, with reposts on LD4all, DreamViews and Reddit [19].
- **Evidence level**: Anecdotal (community) until Aspy's ILDIS (2020), where SSILD + WBTB produced lucid dreams on 16.9% of nights, statistically equivalent to MILD + WBTB; a MILD/SSILD hybrid did worse than either [12]. This makes SSILD the only forum-born technique with a controlled field study behind it.

**The cycle** [19]: three steps, eyes closed:
- *Sight*: attend to the darkness behind the eyelids; do not strain the eye muscles or move the eyes to look for visuals.
- *Hearing*: relax the eyes further and attend to the ears: internal sounds (ringing, heartbeat) or external sounds if quiet.
- *Touch*: attend to the body for tingling, heaviness, lightness or spinning; if nothing, notice the blanket's weight, the heartbeat, the air temperature.

**Steps (official tutorial 2.0)** [19]:

1. Go to bed early, preferably before 11 pm; set an alarm for 4-5 hours later.
2. Get up for about 5-10 minutes: bathroom, rinse the mouth, walk or stretch, without becoming fully awake.
3. Return to bed in a comfortable position, preferably different from your usual one (if you normally struggle to sleep, use the usual position).
4. **Fast cycles**: 4-6 cycles, only a few seconds per step, as a warm-up.
5. **Slow cycles**: 3-4 cycles, at least about 30 seconds per step, timing flexible, do not count; "this step is the most important one".
6. Return to your most comfortable position and fall asleep as quickly as possible. "Don't overthink whether it will work."

In ILDIS the author's written instructions were simplified to four fast cycles (2-3 s per step) then four to six slow cycles (about 20 s per step), with at least four slow cycles required [12].

**The author's own explanation of DILD-like outcomes** [19]: SSILD is "a hybrid", neither WILD nor DILD. The repeated sensory focus "conditions the mind and body into a subtle state that is optimized for lucid dreams to occur naturally". Possible outcomes: hypnagogia during the cycles or on re-awakening (stay passive; "it is better to expect NOTHING at all will happen"); very realistic false awakenings; a DILD where you fall asleep from the cycles with heightened awareness and notice something odd; or a genuine awakening, in which case relax the head into the pillow and do a few medium-paced cycles. In 2011 "nobody knew why it worked".

**False awakenings**: "Perform a reality check after every awakening following SSILD", including awakenings that feel like insomnia; some users lost sleep doing cycles inside a dream [19].

**Common errors (author's list)** [19]: trying too hard and expecting to see, hear or feel things; straining the eyes; becoming excited or discouraged; mixing in breathing, meditation or relaxation techniques; practising at bedtime ("ineffective because early sleep is mostly NREM"); losing sleep; itching (scratch, then do an extra cycle); insomnia (reduce the hours slept before the alarm or the time spent awake).

#### 2.3 FILD - Finger Induced Lucid Dream (Hargarts, DreamViews)

- **Origin**: Posted by DreamViews member Hargarts (often written Hargart) on 16 July 2004, "Finger Induced Lucid Dream (FILD)", with "original directions from LD4ALL" appended, so the technique was first described on LD4all; previously also called HILD after its creator [21].
- **Evidence level**: Anecdotal (community); no study.

**Short version as posted** [21]:

*Setup*: (1) Go to sleep normally; no hypnosis or visualisation. (2) You must be extremely close to falling asleep; the best time is the middle of the night or the morning (morning attempts "can often cause shorter LDs"). (3) Find a way to wake in the night (alarm, intention, waking from a dream). (4) On waking, make FILD the first thing in mind; relax and return to sleep as normal; the goal is the point just before you fall asleep; if you fall asleep, try again on the next awakening.

*Technique*: (1) Imagine playing a piano with the index and middle finger of one hand: press the right finger down while the left is up, then the left down and the right up; repeat. (2) Press so lightly the keys would not go down: "you should basically feel your muscles contract, but your fingers should hardly move." (3) When you reach the near-sleep point, start the movement and focus on it. "Do not count your finger movements or talk to yourself in your head. Also, do not try to make yourself go to sleep." (4) After about 10-20 seconds (the long version says 10-30), pinch your nose with the other hand and try to breathe in; if you can, you are dreaming. (5) If the check fails, do not repeat every 30 seconds; relax for a few minutes and try once more; if it fails again, sleep and try on a later awakening.

Hargarts' transition tip: if you wake when trying to get out of bed in the dream, keep the eyes closed, keep pinching the nose and breathing through it, wait 10-30 s, visualise your room until the image comes, then get up. He notes that the real-world finger movement transitions into the dream (you dream you are still moving your fingers).

**Common errors**: starting while too awake; counting or narrating; lying there for 20 minutes moving the fingers; repeated nose checks that wake you.

#### 2.4 DEILD / dream re-entry / chaining (Worsley, LaBerge; "DEILD" from Klace, DreamViews)

- **Origin**: The practice of lying still after a dream to re-enter it is in EWLD: "Play dead. If you remain perfectly motionless upon waking from a lucid (or non-lucid) dream, and deeply relax your body, there is a good chance that REM sleep will reassert itself"; LaBerge quotes Alan Worsley: "Lie very still - don't move a muscle! Relax and wait. The dream will return. I've had dozens of lucid dreams in a row with this method" [1, ch. 6]. The Course teaches it as "Re-entering the Dream State" (Unit 4, Exercise 4) and recommends it after premature awakenings [5]. The acronym DEILD (Dream Exit Initiated Lucid Dream) and the word "chaining" come from DreamViews; a post by Klace, reproduced on LD4all on 5 March 2007 "taken from the dreamviews forum", presents DEILD as "a substitute for WILD" [22].
- **Evidence level**: Case reports (Worsley, LaBerge) and anecdotal community claims (Klace claimed a "100% success rate if done correctly", which thread replies disputed) [22].

**Steps (Klace, paraphrased)** [22]: (1) Train yourself to notice that you wake after each dream. (2) On waking, keep the eyes closed and stay completely still; the REM period continues; you may enter sleep paralysis, which is natural and harmless. (3) Stay still; lucidity follows shortly; do what you wish. (4) To chain: when the dream fades, stay completely still as you wake and repeat. Community guides add: imagine yourself back in the dream, then stabilise and reality check on arrival; best after 4-6 hours of sleep.

**Common errors**: moving to check a clock or write; opening the eyes; excitement on waking; mistaking a false awakening for a real one (always test).

#### 2.5 VILD - Visually Incubated Lucid Dream (Peter Harrison, "Pedro", LD4all)

- **Origin**: Peter Harrison, posting as Pedro on LD4all, "The BIG VILD topic"; the fullest description by the author is archived in the Wikibooks *Lucid Dreaming* appendix [23]. The expansion varies in sources ("Visually Incubated" on LD4all; "Visual Induction" elsewhere).
- **Evidence level**: Anecdotal; Harrison claimed a "100% success rate with me so far", disputed in the thread; no study. The mechanism is dream incubation of a scene in which you are prompted to reality check, which resembles LaBerge's intention technique (planned action that is itself a dreamsign) [1].

**Steps (Harrison)** [23]:

1. **Design a fixed dream situation** - a short scene, not a plot: Harrison's example is a bare dark-red room with a door; two friends urge you to do a reality-check "competition"; you do both checks (close one eye and try to see your nose; pinch the nose and try to breathe), conclude you are dreaming, the friends react happily, and you walk to the door. Fix every detail and rehearse it during the day.
2. **Bedtime**: go to bed when tired (Harrison: about 11 pm, before midnight); usual snack and toilet an hour before.
3. **Pre-sleep routine**: dream journal and pen beside the bed (Harrison suggests a distinctive ink colour); bedside lamp; read for about an hour, ideally about lucid dreaming, until the eyes are heavy, then push through ten more minutes; turn the lamp off without getting up; lie in a normal position and do not adjust it; progressive relaxation (tense each muscle group from the toes up for about 30 s, release), ending with attention between the eyes and the mind emptying.
4. **Incubation**: eyes closed, think through the scene repeatedly, restarting at the end; shift from thinking to visualising it first-person; let unrelated thoughts go and return to the scene; keep every detail identical. At some point you shift into the dream without noticing; you then do the checks, become lucid and head for the door.
5. **After lucidity**: Harrison adds "shortcuts" (if flying is hard, create a jetpack, then recognise that too is imagined) and prolongation by physical sensation: spinning, jumping, touching the ground, clapping while spinning, rubbing hands on surfaces.

#### 2.6 Tibetan lion posture and throat visualisation (see also section 5)

The Six Yogas of Naropa dream-yoga instructions have the practitioner lie on the right side in the "lion posture", visualise a lotus at the throat with syllables (Gampopa: five syllables, moving attention from one to the next while falling asleep; Tsongkhapa: a small red four-petalled lotus at the throat with Ah or Om at the centre), and sleep with a strong resolve to recognise the dream [30]. Namkhai Norbu's "practice of the night" uses a white A at the centre of the body (heart level) and, "if dreams still do not come", a red A at the throat [29]. Evidence level: tradition. LaBerge notes the Tibetan association of the throat chakra with the regulation of sleep and waking as "intriguing" but untested [1, ch. 4].

#### 2.7 Other WILD guidance: anchors, mantras, Yuschak, Daniel Love

- **The "anchor" concept** is community language for any object of attention that keeps a thread of awareness through sleep onset: LaBerge's own taxonomy already covers it (imagery, visualisation, a repetitive mental task such as counting, or the body) [1, ch. 4]. Mantra-based WILD is simply the counting technique with a phrase, or Charlie Morley's "hypnagogic affirmation", in which you fall asleep mentally reciting an affirmation such as "When I dream, I know that I'm dreaming" [32]. Evidence: case and anecdotal.
- **Thomas Yuschak**, *Advanced Lucid Dreaming: The Power of Supplements* (2006) [34], is the source of the supplement-based "LDS" induction approach (galantamine with WBTB), later tested by LaBerge, LaMarca and Baird (2018), who found galantamine increases lucid dreams dose-dependently and who warn against unsupervised use [37]. I could not verify online the specific WILD or "anchor" wording attributed to Yuschak; the app should quote the book directly or omit.
- **Daniel Love**: *Are You Dreaming?* (2013) covers MILD, WILD, reality checks, dream diaries, CAT and a "dream peg" memory system, with chapters on staying lucid [24]. I could not verify his specific WILD instructions; only CAT is reproduced above.

### 3. Supporting practices

#### 3.1 Wake Back To Bed (WBTB)

- **Origin**: LaBerge's observation (1980) that "certain activities 'in the middle of the night'" (Garfield's sex, Sparrow's meditation, reading, writing) were followed by lucid dreams; concluded that "it is not the particular activity, but the alert wakefulness that facilitates lucid dreaming" [4]. The Lucidity Institute formalised it as the morning-nap technique [3, 4]; the acronym is community usage.
- **Evidence level**: Controlled field studies (NightLight series [4]; NALDIS [11]; ILDIS [12]) and sleep-laboratory studies [13, 14].

**How long to sleep first**: Aspy: alarm at 5 hours after bedtime [11, 12]. Erlacher and Stumbrys: awakening from REM after at least 5 h 40 min (about 6 h) gave lucid dreams in 53-55% of participants (loose criterion) in a single lab night [13]; waking after 4.5 h in a follow-up produced only 0-26%, so "the earlier sleep interruption seems to reduce the lucid dream induction rate" [14]. LaBerge's nap protocols wake 60-90 minutes before the normal rising time [4, 5]. Practical window: 4.5-6 hours, later being better if you can still return to sleep.

**How long to stay up**: LaBerge, Phillips and Levitan (1994) compared 10, 30 and 60 minutes awake (reading about lucid dreaming) before a 90-minute morning nap in 22 participants: lucid dreams occurred in 27% of nap dreams versus 1.3% of night dreams; five times as many participants had lucid dreams after 60 minutes awake as after 10, and "nearly half (over 47%) of all lucid dreams ... took place in morning nap periods following 60 minutes of wakefulness"; they explicitly did not test 120 or 180 minutes [4]. Erlacher and Stumbrys: 60 minutes of MILD-focused wakefulness beat 30 minutes (55% vs 36% loose criterion) [13]. The frequently quoted "30-120 minutes" range is the Frontiers authors' summary of this literature [13]. Aspy's MILD groups stayed up only as long as the technique took (a few minutes), and still roughly doubled or tripled lucid-dream frequency [11, 12]; so a 5-10 minute WBTB is enough for MILD, while longer wake periods raise success further at the cost of sleep.

**What to do while awake**: in every successful protocol the activity was lucid-dreaming-related: reading about lucid dreaming [4], writing out the dream report, identifying dreamsigns and rehearsing MILD [13], reading the technique instructions [11, 12]. Reading fiction or playing Wii balance games for 60 minutes produced 9% and 0% respectively [13]. The Course adds: get out of bed, wash the face, "carefully test reality to make sure that you are actually awake", stretch, then read your dream report noting dreamsigns [5, Unit 3].

**Common errors**: fully waking and failing to return to sleep (ILDIS: keep lights off, quieter alarm, less time awake) [12]; doing nothing lucidity-related while up [13]; waking too early in the night [14]; doing WBTB every night and accumulating sleep debt (LaBerge: "If you find you are losing sleep while struggling to have lucid dreams without result, let go of your efforts for a while") [1, ch. 1].

#### 3.2 Dream journaling

LaBerge's recall instructions [1, ch. 2; 5, Unit 1]: get plenty of sleep (REM periods lengthen later in the night; sleeping later in the morning is more effective than going to bed earlier); set an alarm at 4.5, 6 or 7.5 hours if you sleep through dreams; before bed write the date and "I will remember my dreams"; on any awakening, before moving or thinking of anything else, ask "What was I dreaming?" and hold the question for several minutes; if nothing comes ask "What was I just thinking?" and "How was I just feeling?"; rebuild the dream backwards from any fragment; write key events, verbatim speech or text, and feelings at night, then the full report in the morning with a short title; record even moods and "no recall". Recall targets: at least one dream per night before induction techniques; the Course's long-term goal is two or more per night, with Unit 2 unlocked after seven consecutive nights with at least one dream recalled [5]. Dream recall predicted induction success in ILDIS [12], and 95% of participants in the nap study recalled one or more dreams per night [4].

#### 3.3 Napping

LaBerge's NightLight series [4]: naps were 10 times more likely than the preceding night to contain lucid dreams; "Get Up Early, Take a Nap, Be Lucid" (Levitan 1991) found 67% of participants had a lucid dream in a 90-minute nap taken 90 minutes after waking 90 minutes early, versus 8% in the night; morning naps beat afternoon naps in 9 of 11 participants (Levitan, LaBerge and Dole 1992). The Course's nap exercise: get up and be active for 90 minutes, avoid heavy caffeine, return to bed, set a 90-120 minute alarm, practise the modified MILD until asleep, log night and nap dreams [5, Unit 4]. The FAQ summarises: wake an hour earlier than usual, stay awake 30-60 minutes reading about lucid dreaming and reality checking, then MILD while falling asleep; "one study showed a 15 to 20 times increased likelihood of lucid dreaming" [3]. LaBerge also noted that lucidity "seemed to come easier in afternoon naps" before the morning studies [3]. Evidence level: controlled field studies by one group.

#### 3.4 Sleep hygiene and schedule

Consistent timing underlies CAT [24], WBTB and the nap protocols [4]; the ILDIS found that successful induction "had no adverse effect on sleep quality" and that sleep quality was higher on lucid nights [12]. LaBerge: "Lucid dreaming, at least at first, requires good sleep and mental energy for concentration"; if you cannot allot more time to sleep, "it's probably not a good idea for you to work on lucid dreaming right now" [1, ch. 1].

#### 3.5 Meditation and mindfulness

Stumbrys, Erlacher and Malinowski (2015), survey of 528 people: lucid-dream frequency correlated with dispositional mindfulness, specifically mindful presence rather than acceptance, and only among people with meditation experience; causality is undetermined [18]. Wallace [31], Morley [32] and Holecek [33] all make daytime mindfulness (shamatha in Wallace's case) the foundation of dream practice (section 5). Tholey's description of the matured reflection technique as a "passively receptive focus on current experience" [8] and ADA [20] are the same idea in secular form.

#### 3.6 Dream incubation (Barrett) and lucid dream incubation (LaBerge)

Deirdre Barrett (1993), "The 'Committee of Sleep'", *Dreaming* 3(2): students incubated a self-chosen problem nightly for a week using Dement's (1974) incubation instructions; about two-thirds had dreams addressing the problem and about one-third reached a solution in a dream, some rated by outside judges as objectively solving it [27]. Barrett's steps (as given in her later popular writing): write the problem as a brief phrase and keep the notepad by the bed; review it for a few minutes before bed; once in bed, visualise the problem as a concrete image and let it be the last thing in mind; optionally place an object representing the problem on the bedside table; on waking lie still, do not jump up, and note any emotion if the dream does not come immediately [27]. LaBerge's "lucid dream incubation" adds the intention to become lucid: formulate a single phrase describing the topic, write it down, resolve to become lucid and carry it out, and "spin to your target" if the dream is fading [1, ch. 6].

#### 3.7 Substances, light alarms, apps and cues, affirmations

- **Alcohol and drugs**: LaBerge notes that many drugs suppress REM and produce a later REM rebound; "If you go to sleep drunk, you may sleep quite soundly but dream little, until five or six hours into sleep", after which dreaming is more intense and often unpleasant [1, ch. 10]. The Course asks you not to drink much caffeine before a nap [5, Unit 4]. Cannabis is commonly reported to suppress REM and dream recall; I did not verify this against a primary review in this pass, so cite before using it in the app.
- **Light and sound cues**: LaBerge's DreamLight studies: a taped "This is a dream" produced 5 lucid dreams in 15 REM-period trials but woke subjects 8 times; vibration cues after daytime training gave 11 of 18 subjects lucid dreams; the DreamLight at home was as effective as MILD and the combination gave "five times as many lucid dreams" as no technique; 80% of the time the light is woven into the dream, so mental preparation to recognise it is essential [1, ch. 3]. Any app-based cue needs the same daytime training ("when I see this in a dream, I will know that I am dreaming") [5, Unit 3].
- **Pre-bed dreamsign review and affirmations**: reading the night's dream report and noting dreamsigns is part of MILD after WBTB [1, 5, 13]; Wangyal's third foundational practice is a bedtime review of the day as dream-like plus a strong intention to recognise the night's dreams [28]; "remember to remember" is simply the prospective-memory framing of MILD ("When I am dreaming, I will remember to notice that I am dreaming") [1, ch. 3].

### 4. In-dream skills

#### 4.1 Recognising lucidity and the first seconds

LaBerge: "Novice lucid dreamers often wake up the moment they become lucid" [1, ch. 6]. The Course's table of what to do at the start of a lucid dream: keep cool, calm and collected (restrain the impulse to jump and shout; "imagine what it's like to be dealt a winning hand in poker"); engage in the dream (look, listen, feel); move around (run, fly, dance) while observing; for device users, look away from the lights [5, Unit 5]. Aspy's participants were told to stay calm, perform a reality test, then stabilise by rubbing the palms together vigorously, focusing on the sensations while repeating "this is a lucid dream" [11].

#### 4.2 Stabilisation and prolongation

- **Perceptual engagement (principle)**: all the techniques "are based on the idea of loading the perceptual system so it cannot change its focus from the dream world to the waking world" [1, ch. 6]. Linda Magallon's list of actions that prevented awakenings when vision faded: listening to voices, music or her breathing; conversation; rubbing or opening her dream eyes; touching her hands and face; touching objects; being touched; flying [1, ch. 6].
- **Rubbing hands**: in the Western literature this is LaBerge's and Magallon's touch strategy, adopted by Aspy as the standard stabiliser [11]; Tholey's own prolongation advice is "rapid eye or body movements" when the dream threatens to end [8]. I found no primary Tholey text specifying hand-rubbing; attribute hand-rubbing to LaBerge/Magallon and the general "movement" principle to Tholey.
- **Looking at the ground / at the hands**: Harold von Moers-Messmer first proposed looking at the ground; Sparrow stabilised a shaking dream by looking at the ground and then a fixed star; Castaneda's hands serve the same purpose [1, ch. 6]. The caveat is Tholey's finding that *fixating* a stationary point blurs it and ends the dream within about ten seconds [7, 8], so look at details but keep the eyes moving.
- **Spinning (LaBerge)** [1, ch. 6]: (1) Notice when the dream begins to fade - vision goes first (loss of colour and detail, dimming). (2) As soon as it fades, "before the feel of your dream body evaporates, stretch out your arms and spin like a top"; you must vividly feel the spinning, not imagine it. (3) While spinning, remind yourself that the next thing you see, touch or hear will probably be a dream. (4) Test your state wherever you arrive. Study results (LaBerge's dissertation record): spinning used in 40% of 100 lucid dreams; new dream scenes resulted in 85% of these; lucidity persisted in 97% of the new dreams; the new scene almost always resembled his bedroom; another dreamer arrived elsewhere in 5 of 11 spins. LaBerge's proposed mechanisms: vestibular stimulation facilitating REM, and occupation of the sensory channel. A NightLight study (1989) and a follow-up (NightLight 7.1) tested the vestibular hypothesis. Spinning can also be used to reach a chosen scene by repeating a target phrase while spinning ("Spinning a new dream scene") [1, ch. 6].
- **Falling backwards**: LaBerge found "both falling backward and spinning in the dream to be especially effective in producing lucid dreams of awakening" [1, ch. 6].
- **Inner speech**: repeat "This is a dream ... This is a dream" or "I'm dreaming", silently if possible; Sparrow's version is a memorised affirmation such as "This is all a dream" that must be cultivated while awake [1, ch. 6]. LaBerge's own loss-of-lucidity rate fell from 18% of lucid dreams in year one to under 1% from year two onwards.
- **Shouting "increase clarity"**: a Tholey-style "manipulation by verbal utterance"; Tholey lists verbal utterances among the manipulation methods and reports that addressing dream figures changes them [1, ch. 6; 8], but the specific command "increase clarity now" is community practice (I found no originator). Label accordingly.
- **Staying lucid vs staying asleep**: the Course notes the "other edge of the sword": too much involvement makes you forget you are dreaming; it has you review five past lucid dreams for the events that preceded loss of lucidity ("danger zones") and plan reminders [5, Unit 5].

#### 4.3 Avoiding waking, and waking at will

- **Avoiding waking**: see 4.1-4.2; if you do wake, "play dead", lie still and let REM reassert itself (dream re-entry) [1, ch. 6].
- **Waking at will** [1, ch. 6; 5, Unit 5]: withdraw attention and participation - stop moving, close the dream eyes, fix the gaze on an uninteresting point (Tholey: fixation blurs the scene and wakes you in four to twelve seconds), daydream, or Kedzierski's "fall asleep to wake up" (lie down on a dream bed and go to sleep). Beware of false awakenings when using these; test your state. LaBerge advises against escaping frightening dreams by waking (see 4.5); a good use of waking at will is to preserve recall of something important.

#### 4.4 Dream control

- **Expectation**: LaBerge's spinning results led him to say "I'm impressed by the power of expectation to determine what happens in my lucid dreams", and he recommends starting scene changes small ("Strike the set, change the channel": treat the scene as "modeling clay for the mind", change a detail, then more; or imagine the dream on a giant television with a remote) [1, ch. 6]. Schemas and expectation are the mechanism he proposes for all dream content (EWLD ch. 5).
- **Two kinds of control**: LaBerge distinguishes "magical manipulation" of the dream from self-control of habitual reactions, and argues the latter transfers better to waking life [1, ch. 6].
- **Tholey's taxonomy of manipulation** [8; 1, ch. 6]: by pre-sleep intention and autosuggestion; by wishing; by inner state ("If the dreamer courageously faced up to a threatening figure, its threatening nature in general gradually diminished and the figure itself often began to shrink"); by looking (figures lose their threat when looked in the eye); by verbal utterance (the question "Who are you?" changed figures of strangers into familiar individuals); by actions (spinning, flying, looking at the ground); and with the help of other dream figures. Tholey's cautions: inexperienced lucid dreamers cannot easily produce experiences that contradict waking experience, and manipulation can be used as an escape; aggressive actions were often followed by "avenging figures" [7, 8].
- **Dream figures' abilities (Tholey 1989)** [9]: nine experienced lucid dreamers asked dream characters to draw or write, name unknown words, find rhymes, make verses and solve arithmetic; some characters agreed and succeeded, "although the arithmetic accomplishments were poor"; Tholey concluded that nothing contradicts the assumption that dream characters have "consciousness in a specific sense" and that in lucid-dream therapy they should be treated as rational beings. He also describes an "internal self-helper" figure that can be requested by pre-sleep suggestion and tends to appear in high or brightly lit places [8].
- **Waggoner**: *Lucid Dreaming: Gateway to the Inner Self* (2009) proposes five stages of lucid development and recommends ignoring dream objects and figures to address questions or requests to "the awareness behind the dream"; his frequently quoted position is that the lucid dreamer directs the dream rather than controlling it [26]. I could not verify the exact wording of the stages or of the sailor-and-wind metaphor; quote from the book.
- **Flying, doors, summoning**: LaBerge treats flying as both a state test and a stabiliser and notes that beginners' impulsive flying often ends the dream; doors and spinning are scene-change tools; summoning figures is Tholey's "with the help of other dream figures" and his blowing-out-an-emotion method of creating a figure [1, ch. 6; 8].

#### 4.5 False awakenings, sleep paralysis, nightmares

- **False awakenings**: reality test on *every* awakening - after spinning ("Don't fail to critically check your state after using the Spinning Technique"; LaBerge checks his digital clock), after SSILD [19], after waking-at-will techniques [5, Unit 5], and before doing WBTB activities ("Carefully test reality to make sure that you are actually awake!") [5, Unit 3]. Tholey's advice: try the light switch [8].
- **Sleep paralysis**: relax, remember it is the REM state you are in every night, and either let it pass or use it as a WILD doorway (twin/one/no body techniques) [1, ch. 4; 5, Unit 4].
- **Nightmares** [1, ch. 10; 10]: LaBerge's principle: "nothing can hurt you in dreams", so face what you were avoiding rather than escaping by waking; Garfield's example of enduring the dreamed danger. *Conversing with dream characters* (adapted from Kaplan-Williams and Tholey): (1) practise imaginary dialogues while awake with a recent unpleasant figure, using opening questions ("Who are you?", "What do you want from me?", "Can I help you?"); (2) set the intention to become lucid and dialogue next time; (3) in the dream, stay, face the figure, open a dialogue, address its problems and yours, try to reach agreement or make friends, then wake while you remember and write it down; (4) evaluate. Tholey found that conciliatory dialogue produced positive effects, that attacking figures made them regress (mother to witch to beast) and could produce anxiety, guilt and avengers; LaBerge allows the Senoi-style conquest approach only in cases such as replayed abuse [1, ch. 10]. Tholey's 1988 chapter presents "conciliatory interaction with threatening dream figures" as the most effective principle and reports a preliminary study of 62 students in which confronted figures lost their threatening character [10]. *Redreaming recurrent nightmares*: record it, choose a re-entry point and a new action, relax, imagine the dream with the new action, evaluate, and if it recurs carry out the plan while lucid [1, ch. 10]. On "look for the self": I could not find this phrase in LaBerge; the nearest items are Tholey's recommended opening question "Who am I?" / "Who are you?" and LaBerge's advice to seek the highest value in the dream (EWLD ch. 12, "Seeking the highest").

### 5. Dream yoga and traditions

Presented as the traditions describe them. These are contemplative paths whose stated goal is recognising the dreamlike nature of all experience, not lucid dreaming for its own sake; most teachers state that the practices are meant to be received from a qualified teacher.

#### 5.1 Six Yogas of Naropa: milam (dream yoga)

Dream yoga (Tibetan *rmi lam*) is one of the Six Dharmas of Naropa in the Kagyu and Gelug schools [30]. **Gampopa's stages** (as presented by Kragh 2015): (1) *seizing the dream* - by day regard all perceptions as dreamlike, at night resolve firmly to recognise dreams; (2) *training* - within the dream face whatever arises without fear (entering water, flying) while knowing it is a dream; (3) *blessing as illusory and removing fear* - check for fear and release it by recognising the dream; (4) *meditating on reality* - analyse all dream experience as one's own unborn mind. **Tsongkhapa's four trainings** (Mullin 2005): retaining awareness in dreams; controlling and increasing dream content; overcoming fear and understanding the illusory nature; meditating on the suchness of dreams; prerequisites are the clear-light sleep practice, tummo and illusory body. Posture: lie on the right side (lion posture) with strong resolve; visualisation at the throat (lotus with syllables; Tsongkhapa: red four-petalled lotus with Ah or Om; another tradition OM, AH, NU, TA, RA); daytime meditation "on the chakras, especially that at the throat"; if recognition is too hard, move attention to the crown to lighten sleep [30]. The popular Western summary of the stages as "recognising, transforming, multiplying, dissolving" (used by Holecek and others) is a paraphrase; the lists above are what I verified.

#### 5.2 Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche: Bön dream yoga

*The Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep* (1998; revised 2022) [28]. **Four foundational practices** (daytime, "traditionally called the Four Preparations"): (1) *Changing karmic traces / recognising dream*: throughout the day think "I am awake in a dream" / "This is a dream" and become fully present; check that presence actually increases rather than repeating the phrase mechanically; imagine yourself as a dream figure "made of light". (2) *Removing grasping and aversion*: when a reaction arises, label it "This anger is a dream. This desire is a dream" and feel it loosen. (3) *Strengthening intention* (before sleep): review the day, recognising each memory as a dream, then "develop the strongest intention possible to know directly and vividly, while dreaming, that you are dreaming", and pray for a clear dream. (4) *Cultivating memory and joyful effort* (on waking): review the night; record dreams; rejoice if lucid, strengthen determination if not; commit to consistency. **Preparation for the night**: the "Nine Breathings of Purification" (a nine-breath alternate-nostril clearing of the channels, also taught in his *Awakening the Sacred Body*) and guru yoga. **Main practice**: the night is divided into four sessions roughly two hours apart, each with a posture, breathing, chakra and visualisation and an associated quality (the Wisdom Experience course describes four progressive steps, the first "Peace" and the third "Power" at the heart chakra); the throat chakra is the focus of the first session [28]. I verified the four-session structure and the foundational practices against Wangyal's own articles; the exact visualisations of each session should be quoted from the book.

#### 5.3 Chögyal Namkhai Norbu: Dzogchen practice of natural light

*Dream Yoga and the Practice of Natural Light* (ed. Michael Katz, 1992; revised edition 2002) [29]. **Practice of the night**: lying in bed, relaxed (a little deep breathing if agitated), "concentrate on a white Tibetan syllable [A] at the center of your body" (heart level); an English A is acceptable; the A should be luminous and sharp at first, then "relax slowly but completely" while keeping its presence; repeat if you do not fall asleep; if concentration is hard, write an A on paper and stare at it, or visualise a chain of A's rising from the heart to the crown and descending. If you tend to fall asleep immediately, use an A that is not very bright; "if dreams still do not come, concentrate on the throat chakra, and visualize a red A"; a red ball will do if the syllable is hard to picture. Put a picture of a white A near the bed as a reminder. On waking, sound the A as a form of guru yoga. Norbu states that practitioners should use only the instructions received from their own master; the Aro gTér teaching of a grapefruit-sized white A at the throat within a five-coloured thigle, with women on the right side and men on the left, is a related but distinct lineage instruction [36].

#### 5.4 Alan Wallace: Dreaming Yourself Awake

Wallace (2012) [31] presents shamatha (attentional stability; mindfulness of breathing, settling the mind in its natural state, and awareness of awareness) as the foundation, then Western techniques (affirmations, planning and imagining outcomes, the reflective attitude, dream journals), then dream yoga practices of "emanation and transformation" and the illusory nature of experience. Stumbrys' review calls it "a nice introduction into both topics".

#### 5.5 Charlie Morley and Andrew Holecek (Western integration)

- **Charlie Morley**, *Dreams of Awakening: Lucid Dreaming and Mindfulness of Dream and Sleep* (2013; revised 2024) [32]: structured as Ground, Path and Germination; Part 2 is "dozens of lucid dreaming techniques and dream yoga exercises" including dream recall, dream signs, reality checks, MILD, WBTB and the "hypnagogic affirmation" (fall asleep mentally reciting "When I dream, I know that I'm dreaming"). "Mindfulness of Dream and Sleep" is a programme Morley developed with mindfulness teacher Rob Nairn, later adapted for military veterans with trauma-affected sleep; its listed components are normalising trauma-affected sleep, hypnagogic mindfulness (yoga nidra), de-stigmatising nightmares, coherent breathing and lucid dreaming. His public weekend workshops ("Dreams of Awakening", in person and online) cover basics, dream planning, guided meditations and optional scheduled lucid-dream sessions on the Saturday night. I could not verify the names "Weird technique" or "Columbo method" sometimes attributed to him.
- **Andrew Holecek**, *Dream Yoga: Illuminating Your Life Through Lucid Dreaming and the Tibetan Yogas of Sleep* (Sounds True, 2016; foreword by LaBerge) [33]: chapters on sleep cycles, Western induction techniques (ch. 4), Eastern induction techniques (ch. 5), mindfulness (ch. 6), the "lion's gaze" (ch. 7), illusory form practice (ch. 13), the first stages and practices of dream yoga (ch. 14), higher stages (ch. 15), obstacles, sleep yoga (chs. 17-18) and bardo yoga (ch. 20).

### 6. Training programmes

#### 6.1 LaBerge and Rheingold, EWLD (1990) - the workbook progression [1]

Chapter order is the progression: (1) your present state of consciousness; (2) *Preparation*: dream journal and recall (target: at least one dream per night, a dozen recorded dreams), dreamsign catalogue, goal setting, scheduling, progressive and 61-point relaxation; (3) *Waking up in the dream world*: critical state testing, power of resolution, intention, reflection-intention, prospective memory training (one week), MILD, autosuggestion, PHS, device cues; (4) *Falling asleep consciously*: hypnagogic imagery, pot-shaped breathing, white dot, black dot, dream lotus and flame, counting, twin/one/no body; (5) the building of dreams (schemas and expectation); (6) *Principles and practice*: staying asleep, spinning, inner speech, waking at will, control, getting places, incubation, scene change; (7-12) applications (adventure, rehearsal, creative problem solving, nightmares, healing, "life is a dream"); appendix: strengthening the will, candle concentration, visualisation training.

#### 6.2 Levitan and LaBerge, A Course in Lucid Dreaming (Lucidity Institute, 1995) [5]

Five units; "the first four units each take a minimum of three weeks", so four months is the shortest completion time; EWLD is the textbook; each unit has reading, exercises, record forms and a quiz. **Unit 1 Developing Dream Awareness**: dream journal (bedside station with pen, light and digital clock; note time and date each night; write fragments at night, full reports with titles in the morning), a menu of recall aids (extra morning sleep, intention phrase, bedside reminder, "What was I dreaming?" without moving, alarms at 4.5/6/7.5 h); continue until at least one dream per night for seven consecutive nights, with a long-term goal of two; Dreamsign Awareness (after 10 dreams); relaxation. **Unit 2**: goal setting and a progress chart; Reflection-Intention (eight planned occasions per day, logged); prospective memory development; will development. **Unit 3**: the "I Remember" game; a 20-minute concentration meditation; MILD in three forms (daytime practice, bedtime, and mid-sleep after a dream awakening with the get-up-and-read-your-dream procedure); autosuggestion as the low-effort fallback; concentration and visualisation. **Unit 4 Wake-Initiated Lucid Dreams**: taking naps (90 minutes early, 90 minutes active, 90-120 minute nap with modified MILD, logged); count yourself to sleep; dream lotus and flame; re-entering the dream state. **Unit 5 Traveler's Guide**: discovering your challenges; staying in the lucid dream (calm, engage, move; spinning practised awake first); staying lucid (review five lost-lucidity dreams for danger zones); waking at will; things to do and see; changing the dream.

#### 6.3 Lucidity Institute "Dreaming and Awakening" workshops

The FAQ states that the Institute offers residential workshops in which participants learn the techniques, use the NovaDreamer and take part in research, and that the nap technique is "an essential part of the schedule, one of the reasons why most participants have lucid dreams during the session" [3]. Later programmes under the title "Dreaming and Awakening" have been held at Kalani, Hawaii, typically nine to ten days with 20-25 participants, and included the galantamine research later published by LaBerge, LaMarca and Baird (2018) [37]. I could not obtain a day-by-day schedule.

#### 6.4 Aspy's protocols: NALDIS (2017) and ILDIS (2020) [11, 12]

Both are two-week home studies: Week 1 a baseline logbook with instructions not to attempt techniques; Week 2 the assigned techniques. NALDIS (N = 169): RT only (10+ tests a day), RT + WBTB (alarm at 5 h, light on, bathroom, read a 700-word "what to do if you have a lucid dream" document, back to sleep), RT + WBTB + MILD (as in 1.1). ILDIS (N = 355, six groups): MILD + WBTB with or without daytime RT (breath or hands), SSILD + WBTB, and a hybrid; lucid-dream nights rose from 5.3% to 15.8% overall; daytime RT did not add anything (12.1% with vs 19.4% without, p = 0.052); the hybrid was weakest; recall and falling asleep within 10 minutes predicted success. Daily logbook items included minutes spent on the technique, number of repetitions, minutes back to sleep and whether you fell asleep while still doing it, which are exactly the metrics an app should capture.

#### 6.5 Erlacher and Stumbrys laboratory protocols [13, 14]

Single-night protocols: sleep about 6 h, be woken from REM after 10-15 minutes of REM, spend 60 minutes (or 30) writing the dream report, identifying dreamsigns and rehearsing MILD with the phrase "Next time I'm dreaming, I will remember to recognize that I'm dreaming", explain the technique back to the experimenter, then return to bed practising MILD; lucid dreams verified by left-right eye signals. Results: 53-55% (60 min), 36% (30 min), 9% reading fiction, 0% Wii; 26% or less when woken after 4.5 h.

#### 6.6 Motor practice in lucid dreams

Stumbrys, Erlacher and Schredl (2016; online 2015), *Journal of Sports Sciences* 34(1): online study, 68 participants, sequential finger-tapping; lucid-dream practice, mental practice and physical practice all improved performance from pre- to post-test while controls did not; "its effects seem to be similar to actual PP and MP in wakefulness"; sleep quality unaffected [16]. Schädlich, Erlacher and Schredl (2017) extended this to dart throwing in a sleep lab: improvement depended on how few distractions occurred while rehearsing in the dream [17]. (The user's citation "Stumbrys & Erlacher 2014" most likely refers to the 2016 paper, first published online in 2015.)

#### 6.7 Daniel Love and Charlie Morley programmes

Love's *Are You Dreaming?* [24] moves from the nature of dreams and sleep through reality checks, dream diaries, MILD, WILD, his own CAT and dream-peg memory system, to chapters on staying lucid and on what to do when lucid; CAT itself is a one-week adjustment followed by alternating days (1.10). Morley's weekend format and the components of Mindfulness of Dream and Sleep are in 5.5.

#### 6.8 Multi-week schedules used in studies: what participants actually did each day

- **2 weeks** (NALDIS, ILDIS): week 1 logbook only; week 2 daily logbook plus: alarm at 5 h; MILD or SSILD on return to sleep; optional 10+ reality tests by day; log technique minutes, repetitions and sleep latency [11, 12].
- **4 weeks / 8 weeks**: Tholey's own claim is a median of 4-5 weeks to the first lucid dream with the combined technique practised daily (critical question 5-10+ times a day, imagining the dream state, intention before sleep) [1, 6]. The Lucidity Institute course assigns a minimum of three weeks per unit [5]. Aspy 2020 cites Kumar et al. (2018) as reporting at most 6% lucid days with Tholey's combined technique; I could not retrieve the duration and day-by-day content of that study or of Dyck et al. (2017) because the search budget for this pass was exhausted; verify before quoting them.

#### 6.9 A recommended evidence-informed progression for the app

Built from the LaBerge/Levitan progression [1, 5], the Aspy predictors [12] and the Erlacher timing data [13, 14]:

| Phase | Weeks | Daily practice | Metric to advance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Recall and journaling | 1-2 | Bedside journal; "What was I dreaming?" without moving; extra morning sleep where possible; optional 4.5/6/7.5 h alarms | At least 1 dream recalled per night for 7 consecutive nights (Course), ideally 2+ (ILDIS: recall predicts success) |
| 2 Dreamsigns and state testing | 3 | Catalogue dreamsigns from 10-12 dreams; pick the target category; 8-10 planned reflection-intention tests a day with a genuine question; prospective-memory targets (4 a day) | Hitting most daily memory targets (LaBerge's MILD prerequisite); target category identified |
| 3 MILD + WBTB | 4-5 | Alarm at 5-6 h (not earlier); 5-15 minutes up reading the dream report and noting dreamsigns; MILD until asleep; log technique minutes, repetitions, minutes to sleep | Lucid nights rising toward the 15-20% seen in studies; falling asleep within 10 minutes of finishing; no sleep-quality decline |
| 4 SSILD or WILD | 6-7 | Alternate nights: SSILD after the same WBTB; or morning-nap WILD (60 minutes up, 90-minute nap) on free days; keep MILD on other nights | First WILD/SSILD-type entries; false awakenings handled by routine testing |
| 5 In-dream skills | 8+ | Memorise the first-seconds routine; spinning practised awake; inner speech; review lost-lucidity dreams; planned dream tasks | Lucid dreams lasting long enough to complete a planned task; loss-of-lucidity rate falling |

Rest rule throughout: WBTB and naps no more than 3-4 nights a week unless sleep remains good; if sleep suffers, drop to intention and reflection only for a week (LaBerge's "let go for a while") [1, ch. 1].

### 7. Common mistakes and plateaus

- **Overdoing it and losing sleep**: LaBerge: "One of the greatest stumbling blocks in learning almost any skill is trying too hard ... If you find you are losing sleep while struggling to have lucid dreams without result, let go of your efforts for a while. Relax and forget about lucid dreaming for a few days or a few weeks. Sometimes you will find that after you let go, lucid dreams will appear" [1, ch. 1]. SSILD's author lists "losing sleep" and "insomnia" as its failure modes and prescribes less time asleep before the alarm or less time awake [19]. KingYoshi: take a day off when mentally exhausted [20].
- **Forcing WILD**: Tholey: active wanting dissolves the scenery; be carried in passively [1, ch. 4]. SSILD: "expect NOTHING"; do not strain the eyes [19]. FILD: do not count, narrate, or lie there for 20 minutes [21]. Erlacher's lab data suggest most successes after WBTB are dream-initiated anyway [13].
- **Excitement causing waking**: keep cool, engage, move; spin when vision fades; play dead if you wake [1, ch. 6; 5, Unit 5].
- **Inconsistent journaling / poor recall**: every programme makes recall the gate; the Course will not let you start induction until seven consecutive nights with recall [5]; recall predicts success [12]; write even "no dreams" [20].
- **Mechanical reality checks**: LaBerge, Tholey, Aspy and KingYoshi all warn that an automatic "obviously I'm awake" trains the same response in the dream [1, 8, 11, 20]; ILDIS found no benefit from daytime RT in one week [12]. Remedy: fewer, planned, dreamlike-situation checks with the memory test and the "if this were a dream, what would it be like?" imagining step.
- **Expectation and control problems**: LaBerge's inability to spin anywhere but his bedroom shows how accidental expectations fix outcomes; start scene changes small [1, ch. 6]. Tholey: beginners cannot easily produce experiences contradicting waking life; aggression breeds avengers; use dialogue and looking [8, 10].
- **False awakenings and in-dream insomnia**: test on every awakening; SSILD users have done cycles inside dreams and lost sleep [19].
- **Dry spells**: the literature treats them as expected. LaBerge's remedies are rest, renewed motivation (his lucid-dream rate rose during lab studies "which incidentally illustrates the powerful effect of motivation"), and switching to a lower-effort method (autosuggestion) when concentration is low [1, ch. 3; 5, Unit 3]. Love's CAT prescribes refreshing Stage 1 every few months [24]. Community reports in the ADA thread describe dry spells of one to two months broken by returning to daytime awareness plus MILD [20] (anecdotal). The Course's "Discovering Your Challenges" and "Lost Lucidity Record" turn plateaus into a review of what preceded each failure [5, Unit 5].
- **Trying to escape nightmares by waking**: produces false awakenings and leaves the fear unaddressed; face, dialogue, reconcile [1, ch. 10; 10].
